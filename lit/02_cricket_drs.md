# 02 — Cricket DRS and other review-right designs (NBA / NHL / VAR-FVS / MLB / tennis)

Reader notes for the SSAC27 ABS-challenge paper. Compiled 2026-08-17. Works read in full (or full working-paper text): 11; abstracts/summaries: ~10 more; primary rule documents: ICC Appendix D, NBA Rule 14, NHL/IFAB/FIFA releases.

## 1. Domain summary

Cricket's Decision Review System (DRS, trialled 2008, official Nov 2009) is the closest existing analogue to the ABS challenge system: a *renewable* review right held by the team (only the dismissed batter or the fielding captain may request; "T" signal within 15 s), retained when the on-field call is overturned and — since Sept 2017 — also retained when ball-tracking returns "umpire's call". The academic literature is thin and mostly economics: Shivakumar (2018, JSE) documents 1,201 Test reviews (25.8% reversed; "out" calls reversed more than "not out"; no home bias); Borooah (2016 JSE; 2023 MPRA) decomposes error into umpire vs Hawk-Eye components, defends umpire's call, and shows players make "speculative referrals of marginal decisions" so DRS "cannot eliminate the howler"; Chowdhury–Jewell–Singleton (2024, JEBO) use the COVID return of home umpires (and the simultaneous 2→3 review increase) to show umpires *over-correct* under scrutiny, with the effect concentrated in "umpire's call" marginal decisions. Nobody has written a dynamic-programming/option-value model of *when* to review in cricket; the best evidence on revealed thresholds is from serious analytics blogs (Davis 2017; Bayliss/Red Ball Data 2020; Date/A Cricketing View 2021): batters succeed ~34–37% vs fielding sides ~20–21%; team/captain differences are small; and under the 2013–17 "top-up after 80 overs" rule success in overs 60–80 fell to 20% (vs 28%) — a clean "use-it-or-lose-it" dump, the cricket twin of our 9th-inning dump. NBA (2019–) and NHL (2015–) coach's challenges supply the two other natural experiments we need: NBA success falls monotonically by quarter (65/53/45/39% in 2019-20; 51% of challenges in Q4) and adding a "second challenge if first succeeds" (2023-24) doubled volume (633→1,282/season) while success rose (44→59%); the NHL's switch from timeout-cost to a 2-minute penalty (offside 2017-18; all challenges 2019-20, double-minor for repeats) cut goalie-interference challenges by ~2/3 and doubled their success (24→48%). Soccer's VAR is referee-initiated with no team token; FIFA/IFAB's cheaper "Football Video Support" (2024–) is a DRS-style 2-per-team, keep-if-successful coach challenge. Public data: Cricsheet JSON has a per-delivery `review` object (fields verified: `by`, `umpire`, `batter`, `decision` ∈ {struck down, upheld}, `umpires_call`; there is **no** `type` field); the cricsheet GitHub org hosts only archived converters, not the data.

## 2. Per-work entries

### 2.1 Shivakumar, R. (2018). "What Technology Says About Decision-Making: Evidence From Cricket's DRS." *Journal of Sports Economics* 19(3): 315–331. doi:10.1177/1527002516657218 (WP: SSRN 2700405, Aug 2015; Chicago Booth Review summary). [abstract + WP summary read]
- Data: 1,201 challenged on-field decisions in 126 Tests, nine teams, 2009–2014 (hand-coded from match records). Method: logistic regression / odds ratios controlling for match & team characteristics.
- Findings: 310/1,201 = 25.81% reversed ("conditional error rate"); unconditional error rate ≈ 8% of decisions. LBW = ~75% of challenges. LBW and caught decisions ruled **out** are more likely to be reversed than **not-out** calls (LBW outs 75–182% more likely, per Booth summary) → "when it comes to LBWs, on-field umpiring errors have been biased against [the] batsman." Home and away teams equally likely to win a reversal (no third-umpire home bias).
- Quote: "Of the 1,201 on-field umpire decisions ... 310 (25.81%) were reversed by a 3rd umpire." (abstract)
- Relation: baseline reversal rate & the "who challenges" asymmetry; role-level selection (batter-initiated vs captain-initiated). Undone: no timing/leverage, no token-count model, no optimality benchmark.

### 2.2 Borooah, V. K. (2016). "Upstairs and Downstairs: The Imperfections of Cricket's DRS." *JSE* 17(1): 64–85. doi:10.1177/1527002513511973; expanded as Borooah (2023) "Adjudication in Cricket," MPRA 123268 (full text read).
- Data (2023 version): 1,141 Test reviews since Sept 2017 (post top-up removal) plus 2013 Trent Bridge case; Bayesian decomposition of reversals into umpire error (θ) vs Hawk-Eye sensitivity/specificity (α, β).
- Numbers: reversal rates by team 23.3% (SL) – 34.6% (PAK); India top-6 batter reviews 29.8% successful vs England 48%; illustrative 1,000 "out" reviews at 85% umpire / 95% Hawk-Eye accuracy → 185 reversals of which 42 (23%) are technology error; Trent Bridge 2013: 72 decisions, 6 errors (91.7%), 5 DRS reversals, US$300k → "US$111,111 per point" of accuracy; Hawk-Eye ±5 mm claim ⇒ σ≈6.27 mm ⇒ 1-in-20 errors >17 mm; DRS cost US$60k/day (2011). "Umpire capture": spinners' front-foot LBW success 15%→20% post-DRS.
- Quotes: "DRS cannot, and will not, 'eliminate the howler' because players make speculative referrals of marginal decisions." (p.25); "Unless there is conclusive evidence that the on-field umpire was wrong, their decision stands." (p.5); cites Collins 2010: "Umpiring is about justice, not about accuracy." (p.28)
- Design opinions: suggests one review per innings "to focus minds on genuine grievances" and removing retention on umpire's call. Relation: technology-error decomposition applies to Hawk-Eye ABS too; the "howler vs marginal" tension is exactly our threshold problem. Undone: no behavioural/dynamic model, no match-outcome weighting.

### 2.3 Chowdhury, S. M., Jewell, S., & Singleton, C. (2024). "Can awareness reduce (and reverse) identity-driven bias in judgement? Evidence from international cricket." *JEBO* 226: 106697 (WP: Sheffield SERPS 2023017; IZA DP 16963; SSRN 4526090). [WP full text read]
- Data: 153 men's Tests (90 pre-pandemic Nov 2017–Feb 2020 with neutral umpires; 63 pandemic Jul 2020–Mar 2022 with home umpires), 568 innings; CricketArchive/ESPNcricinfo. Poisson (LBW counts, overturns, umpire's calls), LPM (home win), DiD.
- Findings: pre-pandemic home batters got 26% fewer LBWs (γ = −0.264, p<.01); with home umpires the advantage vanished/reversed; home-win probability −18 pp. DRS mechanism: umpire's calls when home team batting rose 123% (0.21→0.47 per innings), overturns for home batters fell 39.7% (16.8%→10.0%) — i.e., over-correction lives in *marginal* calls. Note the confound the authors flag: reviews per innings simultaneously rose from 2 to 3.
- Quotes: "Home umpires show evidence of a 'reverse' or 'overcompensating' bias against the home team" (p.21); "The 'reverse' bias ... appears driven by marginal judgments, which stayed as 'umpire's call'" (p.24); "Intensified scrutiny may even reverse the direction of judgement bias" (p.28).
- Relation: umpire-behaviour-under-accountability pillar (technology + scrutiny → over-correction; measurable via marginal-zone calls). Undone: no umpire fixed effects on pitch-level location; cannot separate crowd vs monitoring.

### 2.4 Sacheti, A., Gregory-Smith, I., & Paton, D. (2015). "Home bias in officiating: evidence from international cricket." *JRSS-A* 178(3): 741–755 (VoxEU column read; see also Ringrose 2006 JRSS-A 169(4)).
- ~1,000 Tests 1986–2012, negative binomial LBW counts. Two home umpires: away batters LBW 16% more often; one neutral (1994): 10%; two neutral (2002): bias gone. Notes DRS "permits subjective calls favoring the on-field umpire's original decision" (umpire's call).
- Relation: benchmark for "who bears the burden of proof" designs; accountability via personnel vs via technology.

### 2.5 Collins, H., & Evans, R. (2012). "Sport-decision aids and the 'CSI-effect': why cricket uses Hawk-Eye well and tennis uses it badly." *Public Understanding of Science* 21(8): 904–921. doi:10.1177/0963662511407991 [abstract]. Argues cricket's umpire's-call zone honestly conveys measurement uncertainty; tennis presents Hawk-Eye as definitive. Directly relevant to ABS design: MLB's "any part of the ball touches the zone" rule has **no** margin-of-error deference — the tennis model, not the cricket model.

### 2.6 Spitz, J., Wagemans, J., Memmert, D., Williams, A. M., & Helsen, W. (2021). "Video assistant referees (VAR)..." *J. Sports Sciences* 39(2): 147–153 [abstract]. 2,195 matches, 13 countries; 9,732 checks (median 22 s), 795 reviews (534 on-field, mean 62 s); accuracy 92.1%→98.3%. Benchmark for referee-initiated (no-token) review.

### 2.7 Davis, C. (2017). "The art of the review." *The Cricket Monthly* (ESPNcricinfo), June 2017 [read]. Own database, 2,100+ Player Reviews Sept 2009–Mar 2017: 26% overturned; batters 41% of reviews at 34% success, bowling sides 59% at 20%; LBW 74% of reviews at 22%, keeper catches 18% at 40%; ~1.4 batting + 1.2 bowling overturns/match; one-third of LBW reviews since 2012 end umpire's call; team range 23% (WI, PAK) – 34% (ZIM); Steyn 39% vs Lyon 10%. Quotes: "Bowlers are both uncertain and very often wrong about lbws, even after vociferous appeals." "The DRS does not capture instances of teams failing to challenge when perhaps they should have." (= our selection problem: unchallenged calls are unobserved in cricket but observed in ABS/Statcast — a key advantage of our setting.)

### 2.8 Bayliss, E. (Red Ball Data) (2020). "DRS: The story so far," 28 Dec 2020 [read]. Himanish Ganjoo's DB: 3,369 reviews, 327 Tests, 2011–Dec 2020. Fielding side 57% of reviews; success batting 35% vs fielding 21%; 907 overturns = 6% of wickets; home/away overturns 416/413; umpire's-call outcomes 109 (visitor reviews) vs 100 (home). **Timing:** overs 60–80 success 20% in 2013–16 (top-up era) vs 28% otherwise; "teams were in a position of 'use it or lose it', so did the logical thing and reviewed liberally." Also: "I looked for those pointless reviews [at end of innings], but they don't exist." Teams "very tightly bunched"; "how consistent umpires are."

### 2.9 Date, K. (A Cricketing View, Substack) — three posts [read]: "How good are the umpires really?" (20 Mar 2021): ESPNcricinfo scorecard review data (umpire's call recorded separately since 2017); ~10.5 reviews and 2.8 overturns per Test; batters 1.6, bowling side 1.2 reversals/Test; reversals rose from ~2.5 to ~3.0 per Test after the 2016 zone widening. "Is reviewing decisions a skill?" (17 Apr 2021): 2017–21 bowlers 207/1,018 = 20.7%, batters 303/829 = 36.6%; captains' unsuccessful rates 75–86% (Root 75.6%, Kohli 83.1%, Paine 85%); "Reviewing then, is not a skill. It is a matter of having enough information." "The case against neutral umpires" (23 Dec 2021): COVID home-umpire era (45 Tests, 3 reviews/inn.): 644 reviews (164 successful, 25.5%) vs neutral era (48 Tests, 2 reviews/inn.): 430 reviews (136 successful, 31.6%) → reviews per Test 9.0→14.3 when tokens went 2→3. "On umpire's call" (28 Dec 2020) and "The new LBW rule in DRS" (2 Apr 2021): 2021 wicket-zone height raised to top of bails ≈ +12–13 "hitting" deliveries per Test reclassified from umpire's call.

### 2.10 Krishnaswamy, K. (ESPNcricinfo). "Are umpires giving more lbws now than they did before the DRS?" [read]. LBW share of dismissals 17.67% (10 yrs pre-July 2008) vs 17.29% (DRS era) — but spinners 17%→21%, quicks 18%→15%. "Umpires were calibrating doubt with the tools available to them. They do much the same thing now, except the tools have changed." (Contrast: walkingwicket.com claims 14.09%→16.61% using different windows — treat as unresolved.)

### 2.11 ICC rule documents & announcements [read]: 2008 trial PCs (ESPNcricinfo 362178): 3 unsuccessful reviews/innings; Jan 2009 (388205): cut to 2 — Lorgat: "three unsuccessful reviews per innings are too many as there is potential for frivolous or unnecessary reviews"; May 2011 committee: ODIs/T20s 2→1 to "eliminate the 'hunch' that captains often use if they have spare reviews"; Aug 2013 (BBC): top-up to 2 after 80 overs (trial from 1 Oct 2013) after the 2013 Ashes howlers — Agnew: "Paves the way for more speculative time-wasting LBW reviews"; Oct 2016: hitting zone widened (from centre-to-centre of outer stumps to outside edges; bottom of bails); May 2016 committee → 28 Sept 2017 PCs: no top-up; review **not lost on umpire's call**; DRS in T20Is (1 review); Appendix D §3.2.2: 15 s from ball dead, prompt at 10 s; §3.1.3 only the dismissed batter / fielding captain; §3.2.3 no dressing-room signals; §3.4.6.4 not-out stands if impact ≥300 cm from stumps, or 250–300 cm with <40 cm pitch-to-impact; July 2020 interim regs: Tests 3, ODI/T20I 2 "to support the less experienced umpires" (still in force 2025-26 per current PCs — verify); April 2021: wicket-zone height lifted to top of bails so umpire's-call margin equal for height and width.

### 2.12 NBA & NHL & FIFA rule/data sources [read]: NBA Rule 14 (official.nba.com): one challenge; second awarded if first successful; must call timeout; unsuccessful → lose timeout; second challenge forfeits timeout regardless; head coach only, twirl signal "immediately"; called fouls / OOB / goaltending-BI; no goaltending challenges in last 2:00; "clear and conclusive visual evidence"; challenge without timeout → technical. NBA 2019-20 pilot: 633 regular-season challenges, 44% overturned; postseason 67, 40% (NBA, Dec 2020, Spruell: "giving them a voice ... optimizing the overall replay process"). Forbes (Sprung, Jan 2020; n=378): fouls 84% of challenges at 39%, OOB 12% at 76%, goaltending 4% at 64%; Q1 65%, Q2 53%, Q3 45%, Q4 39%; 51% of challenges in Q4; Snyder: "you wanna hang on to your challenges because you might need it in a more impactful time late." Axios (May 2024): 2023-24 1,282 challenges, 59.2%. Blazer's Edge (Jan 2025): 734 challenges, 58.4%; OOB 73.4%, GT/BI 66.7%, fouls 51.4%; Q1–Q3 >60% vs Q4 49%. NHL: 2015-16 challenge needs timeout, lose it if wrong; June 2017 (nhl.com): 131 offside challenges in 2016-17 (+32%), 30/31 GMs back a 2-min minor for failed offside challenge (Campbell: "the close ones, hopefully this will eliminate"); 2019-20 (Scouting the Refs; Bettman: "we only want challenges where it's crystal clear that an egregious mistake has been made"): unlimited challenges, first failure 2-min minor, subsequent 4-min double minor, timeout no longer needed, "missed stoppage" added. Sun-Times (Feb 2020): GI challenges 182 (24% success) in 2018-19 vs 65 prorated (48%) in 2019-20; offside 74 (56%) 2017-18, 97 (63%) 2018-19, 98 prorated (72%) 2019-20. The Hockey News (Aug 2023): 2022-23 offside 89/99 = 89.9%, GI 42/80 = 52.5%, missed stoppage 5/13 = 38.5%. FIFA "What is Football Video Support" & Wikipedia/SI (2025): coach only, twirl + review card; two requests per match (+1 in extra time), retained if decision changed; goal/penalty/direct red/mistaken identity; referee reviews at monitor, "clear evidence" standard; trialled Blue Stars/FIFA Youth Cup 2024, U-20 & U-17 Women's WC 2024, U-20 WC 2025; IFAB expanded trials Dec 2024. VAR proper: referee/VAR-initiated, no team right, no cap.

## 4. Must-cite (in priority order)
1. Shivakumar (2018) JSE — the peer-reviewed DRS review-outcome dataset (25.8% reversed; out-bias; no home bias).
2. Borooah (2016) JSE / (2023) MPRA — error decomposition (umpire vs technology), umpire's call defence, "speculative referrals", token-count and retention design opinions, cost.
3. Chowdhury, Jewell & Singleton (2024) JEBO — umpires under scrutiny over-correct; umpire's-call as marginal-decision proxy; COVID 2→3 review confound.
4. Sacheti, Gregory-Smith & Paton (2015) JRSS-A (+ Ringrose 2006) — officiating bias & neutral-umpire remedy.
5. Collins & Evans (2012) PUS — presenting measurement uncertainty (umpire's call) vs definitive Hawk-Eye; use when discussing ABS's "any part touches" all-or-nothing rule.
6. Spitz et al. (2021) J Sports Sci — VAR accuracy 92.1→98.3%, review frequencies (no-token benchmark).
7. ICC Playing Conditions Appendix D (Player Review), incl. Sept 2017 and July 2020 changes; ESPNcricinfo Jan 2009 (Lorgat 3→2 quote) and May 2011 ("hunch") for parameter-choice evidence.
8. NBA Rule 14 + NBA Dec 2020 release (44%) + Sprung/Forbes 2020 quarter gradient; Axios 2024 (1,282; 59.2%).
9. NHL 2017/2019 rule releases (nhl.com; Scouting the Refs) + Sun-Times Feb 2020 (GI 24%→48%).
10. Davis (2017), Bayliss (2020), Date (2021) as the best descriptive DRS analytics (label as non-peer-reviewed).

## 5. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- DRS is not "2 challenges": Tests currently allow **3** unsuccessful reviews per innings (since July 2020; the ICC web copy of Appendix D still shows the 2017 "two"). ODIs/T20Is 2. State the era explicitly.
- Retention on umpire's call exists only from 28 Sept 2017; pre-2017 an umpire's-call outcome consumed the review; the 80-over top-up ran only Oct 2013–Sept 2017 (Tests).
- "Umpire's call" is a deference zone (centre-of-ball / 50% rules; 300 cm & 40 cm distance rules), not a measured error — do not describe it as Hawk-Eye's confidence interval. ABS has no such zone.
- Umpire-accuracy stats (e.g., "93%→98%") are ICC talking points; the only sourced figure found is Trent Bridge 2013: 90.3%→95.8% (72 decisions). Cite as illustrative only.
- Fielding-side success ~20% is not evidence of irrationality: with a low-cost token and asymmetric information (bowler's enthusiasm) it can be threshold-optimal; Date's "not a skill" claim is about small *between-captain* differences, not about optimality.
- Cricket data cannot show *unchallenged* wrong calls (Davis) — ABS/Statcast can; this is our comparative advantage, not theirs.
- NHL challenge counts differ by source (e.g., 2018-19 GI: 114 vs 182); cite one source per number and note the discrepancy. NBA quarter gradients come from partial-season media tallies, not NBA releases.
- LBW-share change under DRS is contested (Krishnaswamy: flat overall, composition shift; walkingwicket: +2.5 pp) — do not assert a level effect.
- Cricsheet `review` coverage: recorded from ESPNcricinfo commentary; back-fill for pre-2017 matches is incomplete and `umpire` may be missing; there is no `type` field (LBW vs caught must be inferred from the wicket object/commentary); `decision` = "struck down" also covers umpire's-call outcomes (flagged by `umpires_call: true`). Verify coverage by year before use. Cricsheet's official GitHub org has only archived converters — the data lives at cricsheet.org (licence per bundled README: Open Data Commons Attribution, ODC-By 1.0 — same as the Register page; confirm in the zip README).
- FVS trial statistics (reviews, overturn rates) were not found — say "trialled", not "successful".

## 6. Open questions in this domain we could answer (cheaply, with Cricsheet)
1. First DP/option-value model of DRS reviews (win-probability or wickets-runs weighted), mirroring our ABS DP; test the 60–80-over dump (2013–17) and end-of-innings behaviour in T20/ODI (fixed 20/50-over horizon).
2. Token elasticity: 2→3 (Tests) and 1→2 (ODI/T20I) in July 2020 — DiD across formats on reviews per innings and success rate (Date's raw counts: 9.0→14.3 reviews/Test; 31.6%→25.5% success).
3. Retention-on-umpire's-call (Sept 2017) as an RD-in-time on marginal LBW review propensity and umpire's-call share (Davis: one-third of LBW reviews).
4. Role/informational asymmetry: batter vs captain success (34–37% vs 20–21%) as a cross-sport analogue of catcher/batter/pitcher; test whether the party with the best sensory information challenges best.
5. Cost-of-failure comparative statics: cricket token vs NBA timeout vs NHL penalty — a unified "shadow price of a review" and revealed thresholds (NHL GI 24%→48%; NBA Q4 39–49% vs Q1 65%).
6. Umpire response to reviewability: replicate Chowdhury et al.'s marginal-call channel with our Triple-A/MLB pitch-level data (ABS gives *continuous* location, which cricket lacks publicly).
