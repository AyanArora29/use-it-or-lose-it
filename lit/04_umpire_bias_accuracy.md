# 04 — MLB home-plate umpire bias, accuracy, and behavior under monitoring

*Reader notes for the SSAC27 ABS-challenge paper. Compiled 2026-08-17. Numbers are as reported by the sources; where a fetch only gave a summary/excerpt (not full text) I flag it.*

## 1. Domain summary

Fifteen years of pitch-tracking research establish four robust facts about MLB plate umpires. (1) **Errors are concentrated at the edge and are large there**: overall accuracy rose from ~84–86% (PITCHf/x, 2008–2012, rulebook zone, no buffer) to ~92.5–92.8% (Statcast, 2022–2025), but on "shadow-zone" pitches (within one ball of the edge) accuracy is only ~81–83%, and Green & Daniels' "ring of uncertainty" in which P(strike) runs from 10% to 90% is about a foot wide. (2) **Calls are state-dependent**: the effective zone shrinks with two strikes and expands with three balls (Walsh: 3.52 vs 2.42 sq ft; Scorecasting: 188 sq in; Green & Daniels: borderline strike probability ~31% at two strikes vs ~58% at three balls; Kim & King: odds of a ball-called-strike −62% at 0-2, +49% at 3-0), and a called strike is followed by fewer called strikes (Chen–Moskowitz–Shue: −1.5 pp overall, −3.5 pp on ambiguous pitches). Whether the count effect is "bias" (impact aversion) or rational Bayesian use of the count as a prior is contested — Green & Daniels themselves moved from "biased" (2014) to "Bayesian" (2021). (3) **Favoritism exists but is small and edge-concentrated**: home batters (≈0.5–2 pp on borderline calls, larger in high leverage; ~0.2 inch of zone), All-Star pitchers (+4.8% odds per All-Star appearance of a ball being called a strike), same-race pitchers (+0.27 pp, only where unmonitored). (4) **Monitoring and stakes change behavior**: race favoritism vanished in QuesTec parks, in well-attended games and on terminal counts (Parsons et al.); Scorecasting's home bias reversed in QuesTec parks; accuracy improved league-wide with QuesTec/Zone Evaluation feedback, more for younger umpires (Mills 2017); accuracy rises 0.61 pp per SD of within-game leverage (Archsmith et al. 2025); the 2025 grading-buffer change (2 in → 0.75 in) and the 2026 challenge system coincided with a visible retreat from edge strikes and an erosion of the two-strike zone contraction (Clemens 2026). But bias and accuracy are separable — Flannagan, Mills & Goldstone show home bias stayed ~0.2 in while consistency improved 33%. For our paper the umpire literature supplies (a) the state-dependent miss-rate surface that drives challenge option value by count, role, and location; (b) the accountability results we extend to a challenge regime; and (c) definitional traps about "accuracy."

## 2. Works read

### 2.1 Green & Daniels (2014), "What Does it Take to Call a Strike? Three Biases in Umpire Decision Making," MIT SSAC 2014 (2nd place). PDF: https://homepage.divms.uiowa.edu/~dzimmer/sports-statistics/greenanddaniels.pdf ; SSRN version "Impact Aversion in Arbitrator Decisions" (id 2391558). Read in full (SSAC PDF).
- Data: PITCHf/x 2009–2011 regular season, ~1.03M called pitches after restrictions; 75 umpires with ≥7,500 calls.
- Method: semi-parametric model y = p_uh(z1,z2) + ω·x'β + ε; p_uh is a per-umpire, per-batter-hand bivariate kernel strike surface; ω = 1 − 2|p − 0.5| weights state effects toward borderline pitches so β is the pp effect *at the boundary*. Structural logit gives state-specific strike thresholds.
- Findings (borderline pitches): 2-strike counts → P(strike) falls to ~31% (up to −19 pp for some umpires); 3-ball counts → rises to ~58%; 3-2 net ≈ −13 pp. Previous pitch called strike → −17 pp; previous ball ≈ 0 (95% CI −0.001 to 0.011). Threshold: "the average umpire needs to be 64% sure of a strike in order to call strike three half the time" (abstract). "Bias flips about 1% of calls"; "Almost once a game, an at-bat ends in something other than a strikeout after a third strike should have been called" (abstract). "For two of the three states in which the modal umpire is biased, every umpire is biased" (Sec. 4). Time pressure (3-ball counts with steal threat) *strengthens* the 2-strike bias → intuitive, not deliberative.
- Follow-up: Green & Daniels, "Bayesian Instinct" (SSRN 2916929; posted 2017, rev. 2021; under review). Abstract: umpires' decisions "reflect an accurate, probabilistic, and state-specific understanding of their rational expectations" — i.e., count-dependence is consistent with combining a count-specific prior with a noisy signal via Bayes rule. Code: github.com/etangreen/umpires (data via Dropbox link in README).
- Relation: their edge-thresholds by count are exactly the object our perception model needs; the 2-strike/3-ball asymmetry defines where catcher vs. batter challenges pay. Undone: no leverage/inning interaction, pre-Statcast, no accountability treatment.

### 2.2 Kim & King (2014), "Seeing Stars: Matthew Effects and Status Bias in Major League Baseball Umpiring," Management Science 60(11): 2619–2644, doi:10.1287/mnsc.2014.1967. Read in full (PDF mirror at homepage.divms.uiowa.edu/~dzimmer/sports-statistics/kimandking.pdf).
- Data: PITCHf/x 2008–2009; 756,848 called pitches, 313,774 PA, 4,914 games. Zone: |px| ≤ 0.83 ft and sz_bot ≤ pz ≤ sz_top. Baseline errors (Table 1): 18.8% of true strikes called balls; 12.9% of true balls called strikes; 14.66% overall.
- Method: logit with umpire FE; DVs = overrecognition (ball→strike) and underrecognition (strike→ball); status = cumulative All-Star appearances; moderators = career BB/PA, distance from zone edge, batter status, catcher framing; CEM matching robustness.
- Findings: each All-Star appearance → +4.8% odds of ball-called-strike (12.8% → 14.9% predicted for 0 vs 5 appearances) and −2.7% odds of strike-called-ball (18.9% → 17.2%). Effect concentrated among control pitchers (status × BB/PA = −0.507, p<.05). Ambiguity amplifies underrecognition (distance² × status = +0.150, p<.01; ~3 in from edge: 52.7% vs 50.5% error), not overrecognition. Batter All-Star appearance: −1.3% overrecognition, +1.3% underrecognition. Catcher framing (1 SD): +7% / −10%. Count: 0-2 → −62% odds of a ball being called a strike; 3-0 → +49%. Each overrecognized pitch ≈ +0.3% WP for the pitcher's team, −0.067 total bases; underrecognized ≈ −0.3% WP.
- Quote: "High status structures the expectations of evaluators and leads them to 'see' quality in high-status actors, especially when a performance is ambiguous" (p. 2619).
- Critique (Birnbaum 2015 blog): framing, umpire-specific zones and pitch movement/Bayesian priors can generate the same pattern.
- Relation: status effects imply challenge value differs by *who is pitching*; also a benchmark for role/selection corrections. Undone: no monitoring interaction; pre-Statcast.

### 2.3 Parsons, Sulaeman, Yates & Hamermesh (2011), "Strike Three: Discrimination, Incentives, and Evaluation," AER 101(4): 1410–1435 (NBER WP 13665, 2007). Read WP in full; AER abstract.
- Data: WP: 2,120,166 pitches 2004–2006 (ESPN pitch-by-pitch); AER: ~3.5M pitches 2004–2008 (adds PITCHf/x location for 2007–08).
- Method: LPM of called-strike on umpire–pitcher race match (UPM) with pitcher, umpire, batter FE; interactions with QuesTec park (11 of 30), attendance, terminal count.
- Findings: UPM +0.27 pp on a 31.9% base (<1% relative). QuesTec parks: −0.0035 (n.s.); non-QuesTec: +0.0063 (p=.015); "pitching in a QuesTec park reduces the likelihood that a called pitch is ruled a strike by almost 1 percentage point" for matched pairs (WP p. 11). Attendance >70% capacity: −0.0028 (n.s.); poorly attended: +0.0084 (p=.003). Non-terminal counts: +0.46 pp (p=.03); terminal counts: ≈0. Favoritism only on edge pitches. Pitchers respond by throwing more fastballs over the plate when bias is anticipated. Home team wins 55.6% when its pitcher matches the umpire vs 53.8%. Quote: "Umpires indulge their apparent preference for matched pitchers when the pitches underlying their decisions are not recorded" (WP p. 11).
- Rebuttals: Tainsky, Mills & Winfree (2015, JSE 16(4): 353–374) — "evidence of own-race bias is sensitive to specification and methodology"; Hamrick & Rasp (2015, JSE) similar. Snowdon (2021, Substack; BP "Moonshot" coverage) reports ~0.3 pp race effects on mistaken calls 2008–2020 — blog, not peer-reviewed.
- Relation: cleanest baseball evidence that *scrutiny* (technology, crowd, pivotal pitch) suppresses discretionary favoritism — template for our accountability tests. Undone: no pitch-level correctness in main sample; QuesTec was fixed-park, not per-pitch.

### 2.4 Chen, Moskowitz & Shue (2016), "Decision Making Under the Gambler's Fallacy: Evidence from Asylum Judges, Loan Officers, and Baseball Umpires," QJE 131(3): 1181–1242. Baseball section read in full (author PDF at users.nber.org/~dlchen).
- Data: PITCHf/x 2008–2012, ~1.5M called pitches (~900k consecutive-called-pitch pairs), 127 umpires, 12,564 games. "Ambiguous" = within ±1.5 in of the boundary (16.9% of pitches, 60% correct); "obvious" = ≤3 in from center or ≥6 in outside (37.3%, 99.2% correct). Overall 86.6% correct; 30.8% called strikes.
- Findings (Table VII, SE clustered by umpire): previous call = strike → −0.0149 (0.00066) on P(strike); with location controls −0.0151; ambiguous current pitch −0.0346 (0.00217); obvious −0.00265 (0.00094); after two consecutive strikes −0.0231 (0.00108). "umpires are 1.5 percentage points less likely to call a pitch a strike if the previous pitch was called a strike, holding pitch location fixed" (p. 1183); "This effect more than doubles when the current pitch is close to the edge." Make-up-call/fairness explanation rejected: "the negative autocorrelation remains equally strong or stronger when the previous call was obvious … and correct" (p. 1188). Endogenous pitch location biases against the finding.
- Relation: sequence dependence means the *expected* miss probability for a challenge candidate depends on the prior call — a feature our perception model can include; note the discrepancy in magnitude vs Green & Daniels' −17 pp (different weighting: exact boundary vs ±1.5 in band).

### 2.5 Moskowitz & Wertheim (2011), *Scorecasting* (Crown), umpire chapters. Not read directly; reconstructed from Birnbaum (2011a,b blog reviews), BP review, AZ Snake Pit, Mills' SABR chapter, Gentile (THT 2013).
- Claims: PITCHf/x-era overall error ~14.4%; at 0-2 "over 40% of true strikes called balls," at 3-0 ~20% of true balls called strikes; 3-0 zone 188 sq in larger than 0-2 (omission bias: umpires avoid "creating" the K/BB). Home bias: ~516 extra strikeouts for visitors and ~195 extra walks for home teams per season on terminal pitches, ≈7.3 runs per home team-season (they call it >2/3 of HFA — Birnbaum disputes the 10.5-run HFA baseline); bias grows with leverage and *reverses* in QuesTec parks ("only in stadiums without QuesTec"). "If the umpire is going to show favoritism to the home team, he or she will do it when it is most valuable."
- Independent checks: Birnbaum (2000–2009 Retrosheet): home 31.8% vs away 31.2% called-strike rate; HFA 1.2 pp in 8th+ tied vs 0.3 pp with a 5-run lead; 3-0 count HFA 2.35 pp in high leverage; leverage gradient p≈0.1. Gentile (THT, 2013; PITCHf/x 2007–12): 0-0 out-of-zone called strike +0.68 pts for home pitchers overall, +3.62 pts (and −6.53 pts in-zone balls) in tie-game/9th/bases-loaded. Clemens (FanGraphs, Aug 19 2020): 2010–19 two-strike borderline pitches called strikes 52.2% for home batters vs 54.3% for visitors; in fan-less 2020 both 58.6% (n≈330 each — underpowered).
- Trap: popular book, no SEs; leverage claims marginal in replications.

### 2.6 Mills (2014, 2017a, 2017b, 2017c)
- Mills (2014), "Social Pressure at the Plate: Inequality Aversion, Status, and Mere Exposure," Managerial and Decision Economics 35(6): 387–403 (abstract + SABR summary): home pitcher ~7% higher strike odds holding location; each WAR +3%, each year of pitcher age +2%; batters in close proximity to the umpire (catchers batting) get fewer strikes.
- Mills (2017a), "Technological innovations in monitoring and evaluation: Evidence of performance impacts among MLB umpires," Labour Economics 46: 189–199, doi:10.1016/j.labeco.2016.10.004 (abstract; numbers via Mills' SABR chapter): league-wide accuracy 85.35% (2008) → 89.0% (2014) → 89.9% (2015; strikes 87.8%, balls 90.7%); improvements track QuesTec → Zone Evaluation (2009, immediate post-game feedback) and are "more pronounced for younger umpires." 2015 range across umpires 87.5%–91.6%.
- Mills (2017b), "Policy Changes in MLB: Improved Agent Behavior and Ancillary Productivity Outcomes," Economic Inquiry 55(2): 1104–1118 (abstract unavailable; SABR summary): zone grew 30–34 sq in (2008–14), 0.3–0.5 R/G of the offensive decline attributable to ball-strike calls; 18-inch-high pitch called strike ~25% (2008) → >60% (2015).
- Mills (2017c), "Umpire Analytics," in *SABR Book of Umpires and Umpiring* (https://sabr.org/journal/article/umpire-analytics/). Read in full; the best survey to 2016. Also reports 0-2 vs 3-0 zone difference 117 (LHB)/125 (RHB) sq in; Lindbergh (2014): accuracy "largely uncorrelated with postseason assignment."
- Bradbury (2019), "Monitoring and Employee Shirking: Evidence From MLB Umpires," JSE 20(6): 850–872 (abstract only; SSRN 2921553): QuesTec-era diff-in-diff; "modest" monitoring effects, but umpires "quite sensitive to league directives absent technological monitoring."
- Relation: the QuesTec/ZE literature is our closest analog for "umpires under monitoring"; the ABS challenge adds *public, in-game, adversarially-triggered* review, a stronger treatment.

### 2.7 Flannagan, Mills & Goldstone (2024), "The psychophysics of home plate umpire calls," Scientific Reports 14: 2735, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-52402-y. Read (open access). Data: 3,001,019 pitches, 121 umpires, 2008–2015 (Savant). Method: hierarchical Bayesian psychometric function P(strike)=logit⁻¹(−β(d−α)) with per-umpire/count/year threshold α, slope β, aspect ratio, rectilinearity (Stan). Findings: consistency β up ~33% 2008–2015; zone width moved ~2.5 in toward rulebook; home-team bias constant ≈0.2 in throughout — "biases in umpires' decision-making persist even as their overall accuracy improves"; biases act on "decisional thresholds … rather than distorting the perceived locations." Age–learning: 30-year-old improved consistency 47% over 10 yrs vs 25% for a 60-year-old. Replication data: https://osf.io/hv68j/. Relation: gives us the α/β language for role-level perception and a template for umpire heterogeneity; the "bias ≠ accuracy" point is a key hypothesis for challenge-era umpires.

### 2.8 Archsmith, Heyes, Neidell & Sampat (2025), "The Dynamics of Inattention in the (Baseball) Field," Economic Journal 135(671): 2192–2219 (NBER WP 28922, 2021; read WP). Data: 3.2M calls, 26,523 games, 127 umpires, 2008–2018; 84% correct baseline (rulebook zone). Leverage = |WP(home) if strike − WP if ball| (mean 0.014, p99 0.08). Findings: +1 SD current leverage → +0.61 pp accuracy [0.55, 0.67]; +1 SD cumulative past leverage in inning → −0.25 pp; +1 SD expected future leverage → −0.71 pp; effects reset at half-inning breaks; current-leverage effect falls from 3rd (53.0) to 9th inning (16.0); 9th inning 0.39 pp worse than 2nd even controlling for leverage. Interpretation: "a depletable 'budget' of attention" (p. 3). Companion: Archsmith, Heyes & Saberian (2018, JAERE 5(4): 827–863): +1 ppm 3-hr CO → +11.5% error propensity; +10 µg/m³ PM2.5 → +2.6%. Relation: umpire error is *leverage-elastic* — our WP-DP must not assume a leverage-invariant miss rate; also predicts fewer challengeable misses precisely when tokens are most valuable.

### 2.9 Hsu (2024), "Umpire Home Bias in Major League Baseball," JSE 25(4): 423–442, doi:10.1177/15270025231222631 (abstract only). 2010–2019 call-accuracy data; home batters get more balls on true balls and fewer strikes on true strikes; "not entirely explained by umpire, player, or stadium characteristics." Magnitudes not in abstract — get full text.

- Andrews (FanGraphs; Feb 1 2024; May 29 2024; May 5–6 2025; Nov 20 2025): Statcast accuracy 92.44% (2022), 92.81% (2023), 92.53% (2024 — first decline), 92.83% (2025); shadow-zone accuracy 81.8% (2023) → 81.3% (2024) → 82.2% (2025) with in-zone shadow accuracy plateaued ~81.3–81.9% since 2020; ~11 missed calls/game 2023 (one per 3.6 PA); shadow-zone called-strike rate 42.7% in early 2025 (lowest ever) after MLB cut the grading buffer from 2 in to 0.75 in (CBA Dec 2024; The Athletic/CBS May 1 2025; MLB internal grades were 96–98.5% under the old buffer, per The Ringer Apr 8 2026); projected 94.2% accuracy / 8.8 misses per game if 52.2% spring-training challenge success carried over.

- Baseball America (Cooper, Jun 19 2024): AAA 2023 full-ABS vs challenge days — strike% 60.57 vs 62.40, BB% 12.30 vs 10.45, K% 22.61 vs 23.87, ERA 5.18 vs 4.73; 2024: 61.37 vs 61.96, 11.78 vs 11.01, 22.81 vs 23.39, 5.21 vs 4.86.

## 4. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- **"Accuracy" is definition-dependent.** Reported levels for overlapping years range 84% (Archsmith, rulebook zone) to 86.6% (Chen), 85–90% (Mills), 90.8% (BU/Williams 2018), 92.5–92.8% (Statcast/FanGraphs 2022–25), 96–98.5% (MLB internal, 2-in buffer), and Umpire Scorecards' 2026 switch to the ABS zone with no tolerance lowers its own series ~1.5–2 pp. Always state zone definition (rulebook vs ABS 53.5%/27%, ball radius, buffer, projection to plate midpoint) and tracking error (Hawk-Eye ~0.4–0.5 in at 95–99%).
- **Count effects ≠ proven bias.** The Bayesian-umpire reinterpretation (Moore 2009; Molyneux BP 2016; Green & Daniels 2021) means "umpires shrink the zone at 0-2" should be described as state-dependence, with mechanism left open.
- **Home/leverage bias is small and noisy**: ~0.5–2 pp on borderline pitches; leverage gradient p≈0.1 in Birnbaum; 2020 fan-less evidence is underpowered (n≈650 pitches). Do not repeat Scorecasting's "2/3 of HFA" without caveat.
- **Race results are fragile** (Tainsky–Mills–Winfree; Hamrick–Rasp); avoid using them as headline motivation.
- **The BU "34,294 missed calls / 14 per game" study (Williams 2019)** is non-peer-reviewed with an unstated zone; do not cite as fact; use Statcast/FanGraphs (~11 misses/game 2023; ~10.9 in 2025) instead.
- **2025 vs 2026 confound**: the buffer-zone grading change (2025) predates MLB's in-season challenge system (2026), and the ABS zone differs definitionally from the rulebook zone (top-of-zone strike loss is partly mechanical). Any "umpires changed because of challenges" claim needs the AAA 2023–24 challenge-vs-full-ABS rotation or spring-2025 park variation for identification, plus explicit control for the 2025 grading change.
- **Green & Daniels' −17 pp previous-strike effect vs Chen et al.'s −3.5 pp** are not comparable (boundary-weighted vs ±1.5-in band); don't quote them side by side without explanation.
- Chen et al. use SEs clustered by umpire; Kim & King's WPA effects are correlational; Parsons' pitch-level location was only available 2007–08.
- Statcast's "shadow zone" (one ball either side of the edge) is not the same as Chen's ±1.5 in or Green & Daniels' 10–90% ring.

## 5. Open questions we could answer
1. **Does adversarial, public review change umpire thresholds?** Estimate α (threshold) and β (consistency) per umpire × count × year through 2026; test whether the two-strike contraction and 3-ball expansion shrink under the challenge regime (Clemens' early hint), using AAA 2023–24 challenge-day vs full-ABS-day rotation and 2025 spring parks as controls, net of the 2025 grading change.
2. **Anticipation effect (Holder et al.)**: do umpires call fewer edge strikes when the batting team still holds tokens vs when it is out (within-game variation in remaining challenges is a clean, exogenous-ish shifter of expected review)? Symmetric test for edge balls when the defense has tokens.
3. **Leverage-elastic accuracy meets leverage-elastic challenging**: combine Archsmith et al.'s +0.61 pp/SD with observed challenge timing — are misses rarer exactly when tokens are spent?
4. **Bias vs accuracy separability under review**: does home/status bias (0.2 in; +4.8% odds) survive when calls can be overturned, or does it move to un-challengeable states (no tokens left, pitcher-role, 0-0 counts)?
5. **Sequential dependence after an overturn**: is there a "make-up" or gambler's-fallacy pattern in the pitch after a successful challenge? Chen et al. rejected make-up calls generally; overturns are salient, public errors — a sharper test.
6. **Umpire heterogeneity as a challenge input**: do players/teams challenge more (and more successfully) against low-consistency umpires (β) or biased-threshold umpires (α)? Are challenge overturn rates per umpire (B-Ref) predictable from pre-season Umpire Scorecards accuracy?
7. **Peer-reviewed 2020 pitch-level analysis** of home bias without fans still does not exist; a short robustness appendix could fill it.
