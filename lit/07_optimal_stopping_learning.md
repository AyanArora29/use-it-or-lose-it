## 1. Domain summary

The ABS challenge is a **finite-horizon dynamic-allocation problem with a resource refunded on success**: at each pitch the challenger holds a noisy belief q = P(call is wrong) and should challenge iff q exceeds a state-dependent cutoff q\*(s,c) = (option value of the token) / (stakes of the reversal + option value). Abramitzky, Einav, Kolkowitz & Mill (2012, IER) wrote down exactly this model for tennis and showed ATP players are qualitatively and quantitatively close to optimal — capturing ~97% of the achievable value, mildly *under*-challenging, with no learning across 2006–08; two OR papers (Clarke & Norman 2012; Nadimpalli & Hasenbein 2013) solve the same DP/MDP and derive the same comparative statics (challenge more when stakes are higher, when fewer opportunities remain, when behind; extreme favorites/underdogs sometimes should not bother). Classical OR gives the structure: sequential stochastic assignment (Derman–Lieberman–Ross 1972) and reservation-value search (Weitzman 1979) yield thresholds that fall as the horizon shrinks; revenue-management "bid prices" (Talluri & van Ryzin 1998) are precisely the marginal value of a token; real-options theory (McDonald & Siegel 1986; Dixit & Pindyck 1994) explains why exercising now destroys the value of waiting. The field evidence on whether professionals optimize is split: near-perfect play in simple, frequent, quickly-fed-back decisions (penalty kicks: Chiappori–Levitt–Groseclose 2002; Palacios-Huerta 2003) versus persistent departures elsewhere (Romer 2006 fourth downs, ≈2 pp WP/game; Pope & Schweitzer 2011 loss aversion in 2.5M putts; Kovash & Levitt 2009 non-minimax pitch/play selection, ≈2 wins/yr; Yam & Lopez 2019: no fourth-down learning 2004–16, ≈0.4 wins/yr forgone). Deadline effects are robust outside sport (Liebman & Mahoney 2017: last-week federal spending 4.9× normal, lower quality), but for our "9th-inning dump" the DP itself predicts a collapsing threshold as the horizon shrinks — late aggressiveness is the *predicted* signature; the inefficiency, if any, is early hoarding and tokens expiring unused. Early practitioner ABS work (FanGraphs; Oyster Analytics' break-even dashboard; Tango) already reports the Abramitzky fingerprints — challenge rates rise and success rates fall with leverage; low-leverage waste is concentrated in specific players; "teams don't challenge enough" — and explicitly concedes that no one has priced the opportunity cost of a token in win probability. That is what our DP, structural threshold estimation, and rule counterfactuals supply.

## 2. Per-work entries

### 2.1 Abramitzky, Einav, Kolkowitz & Mill (2012). "On the optimality of line call challenges in professional tennis." *IER* 53(3): 939–964. [FULL — https://web.stanford.edu/~leinav/pubs/IER2012.pdf] (see 01 for full detail)
- **Data:** ATP challenge sheets, 35 tournaments 2006–08; 2,008 baseline challenges (2,784 wider), 158 players, 479 matches, 71,555 points. Only *challenged* points observed (we see every pitch — a decisive advantage).
- **Model:** challenge iff q > q\*(s,c) ≡ [V(s,c) − V(s,c−1)] / {[V(s⁺,c) − V(s,c)] + [V(s,c) − V(s,c−1)]}; V by backward induction on i.i.d. Bernoulli points.
- **How they test optimality (the template):** a lower threshold implies a *lower success rate*. (i) Reduced form: success on importance and option-value proxies — optimality predicts success falls with importance, rises with challenges remaining/points left. (ii) Structural: assume the distribution of q on close calls is invariant to (s,c) (Assumption 1); calibrate G(0)=0.295 from 37 zero-option-value points; take ~2% of points as "close" (Whitney et al. 2008); compute q\*(s,c) per challenged point; compare empirical success-vs-q\* to theoretical G(q\*).
- **Numbers:** success 38%; +1 SD importance → 3–4 pp lower success; two vs one challenge left → 5–15 pp lower; each 7–12 extra expected points → ≈+1 pp; a unilateral challenge opportunity ≈ **+1.55 pp match-win probability**; observed behavior captures **96.8%** of optimal value (random challenging 74.5%); players challenge slightly too little (excess propensity −0.064); **no learning 2006→08**; hints of non-pecuniary costs (more aggressive in later sets, lower success when winning).
- **Quotes:** "Quantitatively, players' actual behavior is close to an optimal challenging strategy prescribed by a simple dynamic model." (p. 939) · "There are only two underlying factors that enter this decision: the value of reversing the umpire's call and the opportunity cost of the challenge." (p. 945) · "Any attempt to extrapolate to other contexts should be taken with appropriate caution." (p. 961)
- **Left undone:** per-point choice data; objective perception signal; role heterogeneity; rule variation (two tournaments allowed 3 challenges — not exploited); umpire equilibrium. We have all five.

### 2.2 Clarke & Norman (2012). "Optimal challenges in tennis." *JORS* 63(12): 1765–1772. DOI 10.1057/jors.2011.147. [ABS] — DP over score × challenges left with P(call wrong) as input: "Players should be more aggressive in challenging in the latter stages of the games and sets, and when their opponent is ahead"; optimal challenging reportedly lifts an even five-set match to 59% (model number under assumed error rates — check assumptions before quoting). No empirical behavior.

### 2.3 Nadimpalli & Hasenbein (2013). "When to challenge a call in tennis: A Markov decision process approach." *JQAS* 9(3): 229–238. DOI 10.1515/jqas-2012-0051. [ABS — degruyterbrill landing page] — "multi-chain MDP operating under the long-run average cost criterion" to maximize P(win the *game*); parameters: relative strength, official fallibility; state: score, challenges remaining, outcome of a successful challenge, confidence the call is wrong. Findings: "in some states it is not optimal for very weak or very strong players to challenge calls"; switching in strength is "not 'unimodal'." Maps to our lopsided-score results (a reversal is worth little when WP ≈ 0 or 1).

### 2.4 Romer (2006). "Do Firms Maximize? Evidence from Professional Football." *JPE* 114(2): 340–365. [FULL — https://eml.berkeley.edu/~dromer/papers/JPE_April06.pdf]
- NFL 1998–2000 first-quarter decisions (732 games; 11,112 situations; 2,560 kicks; 4,733 third-down proxies). DP values of field position (IV, quadratic spline over 101 states); risk neutrality validated (TD = 2.40× FG vs 7/3).
- On 1,068 fourth downs where going for it was better, teams kicked 959 times; on 1,604 where kicking was better they went for it 9 times. Fourth-and-goal at the 2: break-even 18% vs actual ≈45%. Cost ≈0.5 pp WP/game in Q1, ≈2.1 pp extrapolated (~one win per three seasons).
- Quotes: "Many skills are more important to running a football team than a command of mathematical and statistical tools" (p. 362); if the failure is imperfect optimization, "trial and error, increased availability of data, greater computing power, and the development of formal analyses" should push behavior toward optimal (p. 362).
- **Use:** template for "DP-optimal vs observed, priced in WP" and the canonical mechanism list (risk aversion over in-game WP, agency/criticism, narrow bracketing) for challenge hoarding.

### 2.5 Pope & Schweitzer (2011). "Is Tiger Woods Loss Averse? Persistent Bias in the Face of Experience, Competition, and High Stakes." *AER* 101(1): 129–157. [ABS; body numbers MEM] — >2.5M PGA Tour putts (ShotLink) 2004–09; par putts made ~2–4 pp more often than birdie putts of the same distance; ≈1 stroke/tournament; ≈\$1.2M/yr for the top 20; persists for the very best, no fade with experience. **Use:** "experts, high stakes, still biased"; test reference-point effects in challenge use (protecting a lead; avoiding the visible wasted challenge) with player fixed effects.

### 2.6 Chiappori, Levitt & Groseclose (2002). "Testing Mixed-Strategy Equilibria When Players Are Heterogeneous: The Case of Penalty Kicks in Soccer." *AER* 92(4): 1138–1151. [FULL — https://pricetheory.uchicago.edu/levitt/Papers/ChiapporiGrosecloseLevitt2002.pdf] — 459 kicks, 162 kickers, 88 keepers; cannot reject equal scoring probabilities or serial independence: "We cannot reject that players optimally choose strategies, conditional on the opponent's behavior" (p. 1150). Warning: predictions "do not survive aggregation in the presence of heterogeneity" (p. 1139) — pooled tests across heterogeneous agents mislead. Directly relevant to pooling catchers/batters/pitchers.

### 2.7 Palacios-Huerta (2003). "Professionals Play Minimax." *REStud* 70(2): 395–415. [FULL — http://www.palacios-huerta.com/docs/professionals.pdf] — 1,417 kicks 1995–2000; 22 kickers/20 keepers with ≥30 kicks; equal-success rejections 3/42 at 5% (expected 2.1); runs tests 2/42; "remarkably consistent with equilibrium play in every respect" (p. 395). Attributes field optimality to expertise, real stakes, and a simple game — ABS shares the binary decision and instant feedback but not the frequency (~2 per team-game), signal clarity, or single decision-maker.

### 2.8 Kovash & Levitt (2009). "Professionals Do Not Play Minimax: Evidence from MLB and the NFL." NBER WP 15347. [ABS — https://www.nber.org/papers/w15347] — "more than three million pitches … and 125,000 play choices … systematic deviations from minimax play in both data sets. Pitchers appear to throw too many fastballs; football teams pass less than they should. In both sports, there is negative serial correlation … correcting these decision making errors could be worth as many as two additional victories a year to a Major League Baseball franchise." Counterweight to 2.6–2.7; magnitude benchmark for our policy-loss estimates.

### 2.9 Yam & Lopez (2019). "What was lost? A causal estimate of fourth down behavior in the NFL." *Journal of Sports Analytics* 5(3): 153–167. DOI 10.3233/JSA-190294. [ABS + landing summary] — 2004–16; 37,103 fourth downs; 13,172 in the NYT 4th-Down-Bot "go" region, teams kicked on 71%; matching estimate: going for it ≈ +1.9 pp WP; typical franchise forgo ≈4.7 wins over 13 seasons (≈0.4/yr; NO 1.5 to CLE 6.7); attempt rate 12–17% with "no obvious change over time" through 2016 — **no learning a decade after Romer**; the surge in aggressiveness came only after ~2017–18 with analytics staffs and public tools (Baldwin's `nfl4th`, CRAN 2021). Coach rationale quoted (Cowher): "that one time it doesn't work could cost your team a football game" — the visible-failure asymmetry that plausibly drives challenge hoarding.

### 2.10 Brill, Yurko & Wyner (2025). "Analytics, have some humility: a statistical view of fourth-down decision making." *The American Statistician*, DOI 10.1080/00031305.2025.2475801 (arXiv:2311.03490). [ABS] — bootstrapped WP models: "uncertainty in the estimated optimal fourth-down decision is far greater than that currently expressed by sports analysts." **Use:** bootstrap our DP thresholds; report clear-challenge / clear-hold / ambiguous zones before scoring "errors."

### 2.11 Liebman & Mahoney (2017). "Do Expiring Budgets Lead to Wasteful Year-End Spending? Evidence from Federal Procurement." *AER* 107(11): 3510–3549 (NBER w19481). [ABS; body MEM] (also in 03) — "Spending in the last week of the year is 4.9 times higher than the rest-of-the-year weekly average, and year-end information technology projects have substantially lower quality ratings. We also analyze the gains from allowing agencies to roll over unused funds." Model: uncertain arrival of good projects + hard expiry ⇒ precautionary saving early, low-value surge late; rollover raises value. **Distinction for us:** the ABS late dump is the *optimal* response to vanishing option value; the loss is forgone early use and unused tokens (their precautionary-saving cost), so report share of games ending with unused tokens and WP forgone from early conservatism, not just the 9th-inning success drop. Rollover ≈ our retention/extra-inning counterfactuals. See also Oyer (1998, *QJE* 113(1)) fiscal-year-end sales surges; Larkin (2014, *JOLE* 32(2)) quota gaming.

### 2.12 Practitioner ABS decision analyses, 2026 (FanGraphs pieces [FULL]; details in 06 — here only the decision-theoretic content)
- **Clemens (FanGraphs, 2026-04-01)** — RE288 stakes: flipping a 3-2 pitch is worth 0.84 runs with R3/1 out vs 0.23 bases-empty/2 out; values a *retained* token at ≈0.1 runs; catchers' challenge rate 1.5/2.3/3.3% and success 63/58/45% in low/med/high leverage (batters 2.4/4.6/10.2%; 55/44/50%) — the Abramitzky gradient; a 9th-inning "spend 'em if you've got 'em" challenge with two tokens in hand; "it's just really hard to be certain enough of a low-importance challenge to offset the value of having one left when you really need it."
- **Oler (FanGraphs, 2026-06-22)** — through 6/20: low/med/high run-leverage pitches are 65.7/25.2/9.1% of pitches but 54.4/30.6/15.0% of challenges; success 56.8/50.7/45.9%; run value per challenge 0.04/0.11/0.23; low-leverage challenges by inning block 708 (65.4% success) / 841 (59.3%) / 992 (49.0%) — more, and worse, low-leverage challenges late; only ~4% of high-leverage pitches challenged; bottom-10 success players concentrate in low-leverage spots.
- **Baumann (FanGraphs, 2026-08-11)** — ≈2.1 challenges/game; team runs/challenge 0.077–0.114 (spread ≈ one win); "teams, on the aggregate, could stand to be more aggressive"; "it's probably possible to quantify the opportunity cost of losing a challenge, I know I'm nowhere near good enough at math to take that problem on."
- **Oyster Analytics (Lane & Riley), "The Oyster Guide to ABS Challenge," Down on the Farm Substack 2026-02-25 (paywalled) + dashboard https://oysteranalytics.com/** — computes "the points at which the expected run value of using a challenge is the same as the opportunity cost of using that challenge," conditioned on inning, challenges remaining, score differential (random forests on MiLB ABS data); claims "teams don't challenge enough," "hitters get the best bang for the buck challenging high pitches," and that players succeed more when challenging to *gain* a positive outcome than to *avoid* a negative one (a framing asymmetry to test). Discussed on Effectively Wild ep. 2444 (2026-02-25), which also links Tango's "cost-benefit analysis of making an ABS challenge" and "ABS challenge considerations" posts (tangotiger.com; robots-blocked, unread) and Athletic pieces (Stark 2026-02-23; Blum/Lin 2026-02-20 on "challenge pitchers").
- **Positioning:** run-expectancy break-evens with a constant or heuristic token cost (06 notes Savant's own constant 0.2-run token cost). None solves a WP DP with the renewable token, estimates thresholds structurally, corrects success rates for selection, or runs rule counterfactuals. Cite as state of practice and as a salience/Hawthorne caveat for within-season learning.

### 2.13 Foundations (standard cites; not fetched)
- **Derman, Lieberman & Ross (1972), "A sequential stochastic assignment problem," *Management Science* 18(7): 349–355** — n resources, sequentially arriving opportunities of random value; optimal thresholds fall as the horizon shrinks — the closest classical model to "2 tokens, ~150 pitches, random leverage."
- **Weitzman (1979), "Optimal search for the best alternative," *Econometrica* 47(3): 641–654** — reservation values (Pandora's rule).
- **McDonald & Siegel (1986), "The Value of Waiting to Invest," *QJE* 101(4); Dixit & Pindyck (1994), *Investment under Uncertainty*** — option value of delay; exercising kills the option.
- **Talluri & van Ryzin (1998), bid-price controls, *Management Science* 44(11)** — accept iff value ≥ marginal value of remaining capacity; our q\*(s,c) *is* a bid price. Refund-on-success turns the assignment problem into a controlled Markov chain — hence the full DP.
- Other sport-resource decisions (03 covers challenges/timeouts): *The Book* (Tango, Lichtman & Dolphin 2007) on leverage-based reliever use vs the save rule; goalie pulling — Beaudoin & Swartz (2010, *Amer. Statistician* 64(3): 197–204) and Asness & Brown (2018, SSRN 3132563; abstract read: coaches "aren't doing it nearly early enough"; MEM: optimal ≈6 min out down one vs observed ~1–1.5); Owens & Roach (2018, *JEBO* 148) career concerns → fourth-down conservatism.

## 3. How optimality is tested from revealed behavior (menu for our design)
1. **Direct DP comparison + tabulation + pricing (Romer):** solve V, classify each observed decision as optimal/not, count, and price the gap in WP; use bootstrap uncertainty (Brill et al.) to define ambiguous zones.
2. **Success-rate inversion (Abramitzky):** propensity unobserved ⇒ regress success on importance and option-value proxies; optimality predicts success falls with stakes and rises with tokens/horizon. We can do better — estimate the propensity directly with Hawk-Eye distance as the objective signal — but the inversion is the cross-check when q is only revealed for challenged pitches.
3. **Structural threshold estimation (Abramitzky §6):** assume q's distribution on close pitches is invariant to state within a role; recover the perception function G(·) from zero-option-value states (last half-inning, two tokens); compare implied thresholds to q\*(s,c); report value captured (their 96.8%).
4. **Equalized marginal value (minimax-style, Palacios-Huerta/CLG):** at an optimum the shadow price of a token is equalized across the states where the marginal challenge is made — test whether the implied token cost that rationalizes observed behavior (inverse optimization; 03 cites Sandholtz et al.) is constant vs. state-varying as the DP requires; and test serial dependence (post-failure conservatism) as the analog of serial correlation.
5. **Causal "what was lost" (Yam & Lopez):** matching/IPW on comparable states to estimate WP gained by challenging vs not; aggregate to wins per season by team.
6. **Learning tests:** trend in gap-to-optimal within season/across seasons, with player composition and umpire accuracy held fixed (Abramitzky: none over 3 yrs; Pope–Schweitzer: none with experience; Yam–Lopez: none 2004–16 then a regime shift after public tools).

## 5. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- **The 9th-inning dump is predicted, not irrational.** With ≤1 half-inning left the token's option value ≈0; challenge anything with q>0. Report forgone WP from early conservatism and the share of games ending with unused tokens; late success-rate drops are the Abramitzky zero-option-value signature (03 makes the same point for NFL/NBA/MLB replay).
- **Success rates are equilibrium objects, not skill.** Lower success can mean better (more aggressive, higher-stakes) challenging. Rank by value captured vs DP-optimal, not by won%.
- **Selection:** q is only revealed when challenged; state the invariance assumption explicitly and test it by role; **aggregation with heterogeneity** (CLG) — analyze within role/player.
- **Magnitudes are small everywhere:** ~3% of a 1.55 pp benefit (tennis), ≈2 pp/game (Romer), ≈0.4 wins/yr (Yam–Lopez), ≈2 wins/yr (Kovash–Levitt), ≈1 win team spread (FanGraphs). Use "≈X runs / ~Y wins per season."
- **Learning needs the right benchmark and a Hawthorne caveat:** no learning in tennis (3 yrs), golf (experience), NFL (2004–16); public dashboards (Oyster, Savant confidence levels, FanGraphs) may move 2026 behavior; separate composition, umpire adaptation, and true learning.
- **DP recommendations carry model uncertainty** (Brill et al.); classify ambiguous decisions.
- **Citation hygiene:** Liebman & Mahoney is *AER 2017* (not QJE); Yam & Lopez is *J. Sports Analytics* (not JQAS); Kovash & Levitt find *too many* fastballs; Clarke & Norman's 59% is a model number; Nadimpalli–Hasenbein optimize a game under an average-cost criterion, not a match.
- **Tennis ≠ ABS structurally:** unsuccessful-only accounting per set with reset vs per-game refundable tokens shared by three roles with +1 per extra inning; the base rate of wrong calls (~6–8% of takes; confirm with 04/06) far exceeds tennis's ~2% close points, so opportunities are frequent and early thresholds should be high.

## 6. Open questions we can answer
1. Full DP with per-pitch objective signals: estimate q\*(s,c) directly from challenge propensity vs Hawk-Eye distance, count, leverage, tokens, inning — not an inversion from success rates.
2. Bid-price test: estimate V(s,c)−V(s,c−1) everywhere; test whether observed thresholds track it, by role, and whether the implied token cost is state-varying as theory requires.
3. Within-season learning under a shared team budget: does the gap shrink, and via fewer low-leverage challenges, role reallocation, or umpire accuracy?
4. Deadline behavior with a *renewable* token: does refund-on-success reduce hoarding relative to expiring budgets? Compare 2-token games, the extra-innings +1 regime, and Triple-A rule variants.
5. Rule counterfactuals as a "rollover" experiment (tokens, retention, who may challenge) with Liebman–Mahoney's welfare framing.
6. State dependence beyond the DP: post-failure conservatism; gain-vs-avoid framing asymmetry (Oyster).
