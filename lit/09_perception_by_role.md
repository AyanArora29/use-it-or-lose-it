## 1. Domain summary

Four actors see every taken pitch from four vantage points, and the 2026 challenge system turns their perceptions into recorded, ground-truthed decisions. The perception science says none of them sees the legislated event (ball vs. 2-D rectangle at the plate midpoint): batters lose smooth-pursuit tracking several feet in front of the plate and finish with a predictive saccade (Bahill & LaRitz 1984; Mann et al. 2013; Kishita et al. 2020), so their "call" is an extrapolation; the catcher sees and feels the ball 2–3 ft *behind* the plane, i.e., a few inches lower than the ABS zone, and is the actor whose framing incentives and prior expectations most contaminate judgment (Andrews 2026); the pitcher is 55 ft away and, empirically, the worst challenger (37.7% in 2026; Reds ban pitcher challenges); the umpire has the best static vantage but is systematically context-dependent (count, previous pitch: MacMahon & Starkes 2008; Green & Daniels 2014) and, per the tennis literature, is at least as precise as the players he is being checked by (Mather 2008). Sensorimotor testing shows individual visual ability predicts *plate discipline* outcomes (BB%, K%, O-swing) but not power (Burris et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2020), which is the closest existing evidence that batter "zone knowledge" is a measurable trait. Every publicly reported 2026 success rate by role (C ≈ 58–59%, B ≈ 48%, P ≈ 38%) is a *conditional-on-challenging* number contaminated by who chooses to challenge which pitches at what stakes; the methodological template for undoing that is (i) Mather's psychophysical model (probability of challenge / of a correct call as a cumulative-Gaussian function of true distance to the boundary), (ii) AEKM's insight that success rate is a monotone signature of the threshold, and (iii) hierarchical/empirical-Bayes shrinkage with split-half reliability (Rosales & Spratt; Deshpande & Wyner). Baseball Savant already publishes an expected-challenge and expected-overturn model and a constant-cost "Confidence Level," so our contribution must be the *joint* threshold-plus-precision model by role with a state-dependent option value, not the raw leaderboard.

### 2.1 Burris, K., Vittetoe, K., Ramger, B., Suresh, S., Tokdar, S.T., Reiter, J.P., & Appelbaum, L.G. (2018). "Sensorimotor abilities predict on-field performance in professional baseball." *Scientific Reports* 8:116. doi:10.1038/s41598-017-18565-7. SSAC 2018 version: Burris et al., "Eye on the Ball: The Relationship Between Sensorimotor Abilities and On-Field Performance in Professional Baseball" (PDF: https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/68d6be744d7efccc2207f571/68d6be744d7efccc2208062e_Eye%20on%20the%20ball.pdf). [READ IN FULL, both]
- Data: Nike Sensory Station battery (8 tasks used: visual clarity, contrast sensitivity, depth perception, near-far quickness, target capture, perception span, eye-hand coordination, reaction time; Go/No-Go dropped for collinearity), 252 pros (141 batters, 111 pitchers; >30 AB/IP) tested 2012–13, performance the following season across Rookie→MLB; league difficulty calibrated on 1,695 batters/1,053 pitchers who moved leagues.
- Method: Bayesian hierarchical latent-variable models (binomial for OBP, BB%, K%; normal for SLG, FIP), league-difficulty α_j and scale γ_j, WAIC model comparison.
- Numbers: WAIC full vs reduced — OBP 1210.8 vs 1226.4; BB% 1075.8 vs 1084.4; K% 1276.4 vs 1284.6; SLG/FIP no gain. Standardized effects with 95% CI excluding 0: Perception Span → OBP 0.64 [0.31, 0.99], K% 0.34 [0.21, 0.47]; Eye-Hand → BB% 0.46 [0.26, 0.67], K% −0.19; Reaction Time → BB% 0.23; Depth Perception → BB% 0.21; Target Capture → K% 0.16; Near-Far → K% 0.21. "+1 SD Perception Span: OBP .292 → .300"; walk rate 7.1% → 8.0% per SD of eye-hand; K% 23.2% → 21.2% per SD of perception span.
- Quotes: "Players who routinely draw walks generally differentiate well between balls and strikes, forcing the pitcher to throw pitches that are easier to hit." "MLB pitches move at speeds near the processing limits of vestibular-ocular tracking, leaving the batter with mere milliseconds to decipher the pitch." "Sensorimotor screenings may be useful for player scouting."
- Relation: the only Sloan-track precedent linking *measured perception* to *zone-discrimination outcomes* (BB%, K%); it never touches umpires, catchers, or a ground-truthed pitch. Undone: pitch-level analysis (their own future-work item), any test of whether the same abilities predict challenge accuracy. We can do the pitch-level version they asked for.

### 2.2 Liu, S., Edmunds, F.R., Burris, K., & Appelbaum, L.G. (2020). "Visual and oculomotor abilities predict professional baseball batting performance." *Int. J. Performance Analysis in Sport* 20(4): 683–700. doi:10.1080/24748668.2020.1777819. [ABSTRACT via OpenAlex]
- 71 pros, 2018 spring-training eye-tracking/visual-motor/optometric tests vs Trackman pitch-by-pitch data; "batting discipline scores" for swings out of zone, in zone, and whiffs. Smooth-pursuit accuracy and visual processing speed predicted league level and O-zone/Z-zone swing discipline but not contact misses. Quote (abstract): batters with enhanced visual capabilities "demonstrate superior discrimination at the plate."
- Relation: direct evidence that batter *zone judgment on takes/swings* is an oculomotor skill; our batter-challenge accuracy is a cleaner outcome variable (truth known). Undone: no umpire/catcher comparison, tiny N.

### 2.3 Batter gaze at the plate — Kishita, Ueda & Kashino (2020) *Front. Sports Act. Living* 2:3, doi:10.3389/fspor.2020.00003 [READ IN FULL]; Mann, Spratford & Abernethy (2013) *PLoS ONE* 8(3): e58289 [READ IN FULL]; Bahill & LaRitz (1984) "Why can't batters keep their eyes on the ball?" *American Scientist* 72: 249–253 [from memory, not re-read].
- Kishita: 6 NPB pros (3 top league, 3 farm), 200 Hz eye tracker + 240 Hz mocap, real pitching. All batters make a predictive saccade 80–220 ms before contact; top-league batters delay it (p = .03, η² = .69) and track marginally better in the final 300 ms (p = .06). "It is presumably impossible to track the ball during its entire flight" (angular velocity >800°/s). No swing/take contrast.
- Mann et al.: 2 elite vs 2 club cricketers; elite keep head–ball angle 2.4° ± 1.7° vs 3.7° ± 1.4°, use two predictive saccades (bounce, contact), and have gaze on the ball at contact in 100% of good-length trials vs 13% for club. Quotes the received view that "batters do not – and possibly cannot – watch the ball at the moment they hit it."
- Bahill & LaRitz (memory): a pro tracked the ball with smooth pursuit until ≈5.5 ft in front of the plate; eye-velocity limits (~120°/s pursuit) mean the last ~40 ms are extrapolated.
- Relation/our use: the batter's challenge is a judgment about a plane he never foveates; the last ~6 ft contain roughly a quarter of a breaking ball's total movement (movement grows ~quadratically with distance), i.e., ≈1–2 in of unresolved horizontal/vertical displacement — same order as the shadow-zone width where challenges live. Cite as motivation for a role-specific perceptual SD, not as a measured baseball number.

### 2.4 MacMahon, C., & Starkes, J.L. (2008). "Contextual influences on baseball ball-strike decisions in umpires, players, and controls." *J. Sports Sciences* 26(7): 751–760. doi:10.1080/02640410701813050. [ABSTRACT via OpenAlex; 50–53 citations]
- Video-clip ball/strike judgments by pro umpires, players, and inexperienced controls. Umpires and players "significantly outperformed controls" on basic accuracy; borderline pitches preceded by definite strikes were judged closer to the boundary; counts favoring a strike (3-0, 3-2) shifted judgments toward strike; context effects appeared even in controls; "umpires demonstrated greater propensity to call strikes," which the authors relate to a "hastening the game" convention.
- Relation: the only controlled comparison of umpires vs players on the *same* stimuli — accuracy similar between the two expert groups, and both are context-biased. This is the perceptual foundation for the count-dependent bias Green & Daniels (2014) measured in the field and for treating count as a *criterion* shifter in our SDT model (it shifts everyone's criterion, including the challenger's). Undone: no batter-vs-catcher split, no field data, no stakes.

### 2.5 Umpire vantage & gaze — Millslagle, Hines & Smith (2013). "Quiet eye gaze behavior of expert, and near-expert, baseball plate umpires." *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 116(1): 69–77. doi:10.2466/24.22.27.PMS.116.1.69-77 [ABSTRACT via Crossref]; Ford, Goodwin & Richardson (1996) *Int. J. Sport Psychology* 27: 50–58 and Ford, Gallagher, Lacy, Bridwell & Goodwin (1999) *J. Sport Behavior* 22(1): 28–44 [FROM MEMORY — NOT VERIFIED; do not quote].
- Millslagle: 4 expert vs 4 near-expert umpires, eye tracking across preparation/delivery/flight/call; experts fixate the release area earlier and longer and track the ball longer — "the area outside the pitcher's ball release point may be the key environmental cue for the behind-the-plate umpire."
- Ford et al. (memory): video-perspective experiments showing ball/strike accuracy depends on the umpire's head position (AL "over the top" vs NL "slot"/lower), with position-specific blind spots on low/outside pitches. Use only as a pointer that vantage (and the catcher's body) is a known accuracy determinant; verify before citing.
- Relation: umpire precision is location-specific (worst at top/bottom edges and low-away; cf. Zhan et al. 2020, Roegele in 04/06). In a role model, umpire error density by location is the *opportunity set* that batters and catchers face; the catcher's set-up partly *causes* umpire error (framing) and partly obstructs the umpire's own view.

### 2.6 Mather (2008) *Proc. R. Soc. B* 275: 1645–1651 and Whitney et al. (2008) *Current Biology* 18: R947–R949 — see 01 for details. Use here as the psychophysical template: challenge probability (and official's error probability) as a cumulative Gaussian in true distance from the boundary; estimated positional SD < 40 mm for both players and line judges, judges more reliable; officials' errors are directional (84% in the direction of motion). Our analogue: fit P(challenge | d, role, state) and P(umpire wrong | location) with role/umpire-specific σ; test whether umpire errors are directional in the count/handedness sense.

### 2.7 Abramitzky, Einav, Kolkowitz & Mill (2012), *IER* 53(3): 939–964 — the selection template. [READ IN FULL, Stanford PDF] (model details in 01/07)
- Selection problem stated on p. 942: "We do not have point-by-point data on points that were not challenged." Their fix: parameterize G(y) = E(q | q > y); Assumption 1 (belief distribution invariant to state); then "a higher propensity to challenge implies challenging less conservatively, thereby leading to lower success rates," so success rates *identify* threshold movements without seeing unchallenged points. Reduced form: +1 SD importance → 3–4 pp lower success; +1 challenge remaining → 5–15 pp lower success; late-set 34.9% vs early 40.5%; tiebreak 26.5%. Structural: calibrate G(0) = 0.295 from 37 zero-option-value points, p_close = 2% (Whitney), linear G, back out q\* per challenge; observed captures 96.8% of optimal value; players under-challenge by ≈6.4 pp.
- Relation: this is exactly why 2026 role rates cannot be read as perception; **but our data are better** — every taken pitch has a Hawk-Eye location, so the unchallenged denominator is observed and Assumption 1 becomes testable (does the distribution of true miss distances on challenged pitches change with stakes/tokens?). Undone by AEKM: no perception model, one decision-maker.

### 2.8 Deshpande, S.K., & Wyner, A.J. (2017). "A hierarchical Bayesian model of pitch framing." *JQAS* 13(3): 95–112; arXiv:1704.00823 [READ IN FULL] and Rosales & Spratt (2015) SSAC "Who Is Responsible for a Called Strike?" [full read in 05].
- D&W: 2014, N = 308,388 taken pitches within 1 ft of zone; 93 umpires, 101 catchers, 719 pitchers, 1,010 batters; umpire-specific intercept and slope on a historical-GAM location log-odds, common count/catcher/pitcher/batter effects (Model 3 wins out of sample; fully umpire-specific effects overfit). Catcher effects ≈ count effects in magnitude ([−1.5, 1.5] log-odds); "with a single season's worth of data, we cannot discriminate between good framers with the same certainty that we can separate good framers from bad framers"; year-to-year CAFE r = 0.71 → 0.58 (2014–15).
- R&S (from 05): even/odd-year reliability catcher 0.86, umpire 0.77, batter 0.50, pitcher 0.46; batter leader ≈ +3 runs; 0.1189 runs per strike.
- Relation: gives (i) the four-actor random-effects architecture we re-use with a *known truth* per pitch (so the outcome is "call correct?" or "challenge correct?" rather than "called strike?"), (ii) the reliability-by-role benchmarks (batter/pitcher effects on calls are real but half as reliable as catcher effects), (iii) the warning that single-season player effects need shrinkage and interval reporting.

### 2.9 Baseball Savant ABS metrics documentation, dashboard, leaderboard (2026). https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/abs-metrics-documentation ; https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/abs ; https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/leaderboard/abs-challenges [READ IN FULL]
- Definitions (verbatim): "Challenge Opportunity" = "Any pitch where a player is allowed, by the rulebook, to make a challenge" (called pitch, ≥1 challenge remaining, call against you; ≈50% of pitches). "Reasonable Pitch" = opportunity where "The umpire's call was mistaken," or "was a correct call, yet within 3 inches (i.e., close) and had a run value of at least +0.30, via the RE288 system," or ≥20% challenge probability (≈5% of pitches). "Confidence Level": "The breakeven point of a challenge is `[0.2 / 0.2 + run value of situation]`. The breakeven point is the confidence level needed." "A run value of 0.3 runs means the breakeven point is 40%." Buckets on the leaderboard: Low (<40%), 50/50 (40–60%), High (60–80%), Very High (>80%). Framing vs challenge: "the umpire's initial call is used to establish the run value of catcher framing"; "The difference between the original and final call is considered a separate 'challenge skill' run value"; "Flipping from a called ball to a final strike earns +1.00 towards his challenging skill."
- Leaderboard: filters by role (batters/catchers/pitchers/fielding team/team), season 2025–26, MLB and AAA, pitch type, attack zone, confidence bucket, in/out of zone; CSV includes exp_chal, **exp_rate_overturns**, exp_rate_challenges (expected-challenge model "including pitch location, remaining challenges, runners on base, and ball/strike/out situations"). Dashboard, Aug 17 2026: 7,974 attempts, 54% overturned; batters 48% (1,777/3,666), fielders 58% (2,498/4,308); 06 tallies pitchers 52/138 = 37.7%.
- Relation: MLB itself already publishes (a) a role split, (b) an expected-overturn baseline (the "conditional on location" correction), (c) a constant-token-cost breakeven. Our advances: replace 0.2 with the DP's state-dependent option value; model *precision and threshold jointly* rather than reporting success-above-expected; publish uncertainty (shrinkage) per player.

### 2.11 UmpScorecards (Ethan Singer), explainer https://umpscorecards.com/explainers/ [READ]
- Accuracy = "the percent of called pitches called correctly by the umpire"; xAcc = "the expected number of correct calls divided by the total number of calls" from location-based algorithms; AAx = actual − expected; consistency; favor; uses plate_x/plate_z and sz_top/sz_bot. ### 2.12 Plate-discipline / zone-knowledge metrics [FROM MEMORY; verify definitions before citing]
- Statcast Swing/Take (Tango, 2019): attack zones heart/shadow/chase/waste (shadow ≈ one ball width either side of the zone edge), run value credited to swings and takes by zone; takes in the shadow zone are where batter zone knowledge is expressed. ### 2.13 Statistical toolkit (standard references, from memory)
- Signal-detection theory (Green & Swets 1966): decision = signal (true miss distance d + N(0, σ_role²)) vs criterion c(state); P(challenge | d) = Φ((d − c)/σ) identifies σ and c *from the decision curve alone*; success rate is P(d > 0 | challenge), a derived quantity. Item-response/Rasch view: pitch difficulty (|d|) × person ability. Probit/logit with crossed random effects (player, umpire, count) = Deshpande–Wyner architecture. Empirical Bayes/shrinkage: Efron & Morris (1975, JASA); reliability via split-half/even-odd (Rosales & Spratt) or KR-21 stabilization (Carleton, BP). Selection: Heckman (1979) needs an exclusion restriction — stakes and tokens remaining shift the challenge decision but cannot change whether the umpire was wrong, so they are natural instruments; Manski-style bounds for the never-challenged region; AEKM's success-as-threshold-proxy under invariance.

## 4. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- "Catchers perceive the zone best" is not established: 58% vs 48% vs 38% are conditional on self-selected challenges at different distances, counts and stakes, under team policies that restrict who may challenge (Reds bar pitchers; Orioles route to catchers). Report role differences only after conditioning on true miss distance and state, with shrinkage; call the raw gap "success rate," not "accuracy."
- The catcher's evidence is displaced from the legislated plane (glove 2–3 ft behind mid-plate → ball ≈2–5 in lower depending on VAA); the batter's is truncated ~5 ft in front. State these as physics/psychophysics motivation, not as measured effects, unless we estimate them.
- Andrews' "10 pp worse where they frame well" is early-season, ~40 catchers, no CIs, and confounded by opportunity quality; treat as hypothesis.
- MacMahon & Starkes used video clips, not live pitches; do not say "umpires and players are equally accurate in games."
- Mather's <40 mm and "judges more reliable than players" are tennis; the ABS zone edge is a 3-D-to-2-D projection with batter-height dependence — do not transplant the SD.
- Burris/Liu predict season-level BB%/K% and swing-discipline scores, not called-strike judgment; the "vision predicts zone knowledge" claim needs our pitch-level test.
- Savant's 0.2-run token cost is a convention (docs); its "expected overturn" model is undocumented in detail — cite as a baseline, do not treat as ground truth for the option value.
- Rosales & Spratt reliabilities are for called-strike *influence* (framing-type effects), not for perception; use as a prior on how noisy single-season batter/pitcher effects will be, not as evidence about challenge skill.
- Statcast coordinate/zone definitions changed in 2026 (mid-plate plate_x/z, ABS sz_top/sz_bot; see 06) — any 2025 vs 2026 accuracy-by-role comparison must re-derive location at the same plane.
- Multiple actors per defensive challenge: the "catcher" success rate is really the defense's, occasionally with dugout/pitcher input; treat defense as a composite decision-maker unless the challenger ID is reliable.

## 5. Open questions we can answer
1. Role-specific perceptual precision σ_role and criterion c(state) from P(challenge | d, state) using every taken pitch (unchallenged denominator observed) — the first field estimate of "who sees the zone best," with a direct test of AEKM's Assumption 1 (does the challenged-d distribution shift with stakes?).
2. Is the pitcher's 38% low precision or bad selection (they challenge farther-off pitches, at higher stakes)? Same for batters vs catchers; decompose success gap into precision, threshold, and opportunity components.
3. Location- and side-specific σ (top/bottom vs sides; glove/arm side; batter height) and the Andrews framing–over-confidence interaction, estimated with shrinkage and CIs — feeds the framing/challenge equilibrium.
4. Does challenge skill correlate with existing skills — Statcast framing runs (catchers), chase rate/shadow-zone take value/SEAGER (batters), command (pitchers) — and does it stabilize (split-half, year-to-year 2025 AAA→2026 MLB for promoted players)?
5. Umpire precision by location vs the batter's/catcher's: is the checked party more precise than the checkers (tennis says yes for judges)? Compare umpire error curve σ_ump(location) with σ_role from challenge decisions.
6. Sensorimotor link at pitch level: for players with public vision-test data (none), no; but batter age/experience/handedness × side of plate as observable proxies.
7. Learning: does σ or c move within 2026 (first 30 vs last 30 team-games), and does AAA 2025 experience predict MLB 2026 precision at the player level (Savant has AAA rows)?
