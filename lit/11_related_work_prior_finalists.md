## 1. Domain summary

Across 2012–2025 SSAC finalists, exactly three papers touch called balls and strikes — Green & Daniels (2014, umpire count/previous-call biases via structural strike thresholds), Rosales & Spratt (2015, Strike Zone Plus/Minus that splits called-strike credit across catcher, pitcher, batter, umpire) and Zhan, Gerstner & Polimeni (2020, RE24 cost of missed calls under a hypothetical robo-ump, fixed zone). None models a challenge, a token, a decision threshold, or the ABS zone; Rosales & Spratt even wrote that "balls and strikes are notable exclusions to the list of reviewable plays" and that "the strike zone will likely remain the final stronghold of the umpire's influence over the game" — a sentence we can quote as the world our paper overturns. The 2026 finalist/poster lists (fetched Aug 17, 2026) contain no umpire/ABS paper: the closest are a boxing-judging finalist (7,323 rounds; officiating-measurement) and a Baseball-track poster on pickoffs by the same BYU group that produced the 2023 and 2024 baseball finalists. So the field is open. On *form*, the four winners we read (2013 roster flexibility, 2020 no-tanking draft, 2024 darts MDP, 2025 penalty kicks) share a rhythm: open with an institutional fact and a number; state a crisp gap ("heuristics that are not proven"; "there has not been a systematic theoretical evaluation"); one sentence "In this paper, we develop…"; a numbered 3-item contributions list; a model (MDP/LP/game) *plus* data *plus* a rule/policy counterfactual; numbers in the abstract; a short conclusion that names what the paper does *not* claim; code on GitHub. Two of the four winners (2020, 2024) are theory-first with modest data; the 2025 winner has n = 536. Our DP + revealed thresholds + rule counterfactuals + open dataset fits the winning template exactly; the risk is over-length and over-claiming, which the style rules below address.

## 2. Per-work entries

### 2.1 Green & Daniels (2014) — "What Does it Take to Call a Strike? Three Biases in Umpire Decision Making" (Stanford). READ IN FULL. ~3,100 words.
- Abstract (verbatim, key sentences): "We analyze all regular season calls from 2009 to 2011—over one million pitches—using non-parametric and structural estimation methods. We find that the strike zone contracts in 2-strike counts and expands in 3-ball counts, and that umpires are reluctant to call two strikes in a row. Effect sizes can be dramatic: in 2-strike counts the probability of a called strike drops by as much as 19 percentage points in the corners of the strike zone. … If an umpire is unbiased, he would only need to be 50% sure that a pitch is a strike in order to call a strike half the time. In fact, the average umpire needs to be 64% sure of a strike in order to call strike three half the time. Moreover, the least biased umpire still needs to be 55% sure … In other words, every umpire is biased."
- First paragraph: opens with the behavioral-economics literature on athletes (Berger & Pope 2011; Pope & Schweitzer 2011; Pope & Simonsohn 2011), then the pivot: "We study a context in which any deviations from rationality are clearly suboptimal: the calls of home plate umpires." Second paragraph ends with the game-level translation: "Bias flips about 1% of calls. Almost once a game, an at-bat ends in something other than a strikeout after a third strike should have been called."
- Data: PITCHf/x 2009–11, front-of-plate location, vertical axis normalized by operator-set batter zone; 75 umpires with ≥7,500 calls, 1.03M calls.
- Method: bivariate Gaussian kernel regression (Silverman bandwidth) of P(strike | location); state-difference "mountain/moat" plots; semi-parametric model y = p_uh(z1,z2) + ω·x'β with borderline weight ω = 1 − 2|p − 0.5| (Appendix A); structural utility model with per-umpire disutilities γ_s, γ_b for wrong strikes/balls, logit, simulated "strike threshold" (Appendix B).
- Numbers: 3-ball → borderline strike prob 58%; 2-strike → 31%; called strike on last pitch → −17 pp; full count → −13 pp; time-pressure calls (≈2%) deepen the 2-strike moat → bias is "intuitive" not deliberative; modal thresholds 60% (last pitch strike), 35% (3 balls), >60% (2 strikes); "the ring in which the probability of a strike rises from 10% to 90% is a foot wide."
- Contributions/limitations: no formal lists; explicit caveats: "causal identification rests on an assumption of exogeneity with respect to omitted states"; "An alternate framework could allow for biased beliefs instead." Exhibits: Figures 1a–b, 2, 3a–b, 4; Table 1; Appendices A–B. No prior SSAC citations.
- Relation: their strike-threshold construct is the umpire-side twin of our challenge threshold; count-dependent umpire bias predicts count-dependent challenge value; the "foot-wide ring" is where all challenges live. Left undone: no accountability regime, no ABS ground truth (they use umpire-consensus location), nothing after 2011.

### 2.2 Rosales & Spratt (2015) — "Who Is Responsible For A Called Strike?" (Baseball Info Solutions). READ IN FULL. ~5,100 words.
- Abstract (verbatim, core): "…we attempt to divide the credit for whether a pitch is called a ball or strike among the catcher, the pitcher, the batter, and the umpire involved. We call our system Strike Zone Plus/Minus, and it is unique … in two ways. First, we treat pitchers, batters, and umpires as independent actors in the system rather than treating them as variables to adjust the catcher's performance by. Second, we use Baseball Info Solutions data on where the catcher sets his target …"
- Data: BIS pitch data 2010–13 (buckets), results for 2014; catcher-target charting from 2010.
- Method: expected strike % by 1-inch location grid × count × command (horizontal distance to target) × batter handedness; iterative credit split among four actors (~10 iterations to converge); regression toward 250 league-average pitches; run value 0.1189 runs per ball→strike (2010–13 RE by count). Validation: "with or without you" correlations (pitcher consistency across catchers 0.80/0.91; catcher-vs-other-catchers −0.04/−0.03; naive equal split gives 0.19/0.19); even/odd-year correlations P 0.46, C 0.86, B 0.50, U 0.77.
- Numbers: 3-0 count +5.6% strike prob, 0-1 −3.2%; command group 4 (>10.4 in from target) −8.6%; 2014 leaders Zunino/Conger +16 SZRS, Navarro −17; umpires Kulpa/Miller/Eddings +12, Schrieber −16; top catcher 16 runs vs 24 in Carruth and Pavlidis–Brooks; "Sixteen runs is worth about $11–12 million."
- Quotable: "the strike zone will likely remain the final stronghold of the umpire's influence over the game" (Conclusion). Exhibits: Tables 1–13, no figures. Prior work cited: Carruth (StatCorner), Fast (BP 2011), Pavlidis & Brooks (BP 2014) — no prior SSAC cites; no limitations section.
- Relation: the four-actor decomposition is our role model for perception-by-role (catcher/batter/pitcher/umpire) — but they decompose *influence on the call*, we decompose *accuracy of the challenge decision*. Left undone: nothing about review, nothing about what happens to framing value when the borderline call can be reversed (our framing/challenge equilibrium).

### 2.3 Zhan, Gerstner & Polimeni (2020) — "Measuring the Impact of Robotic Umpires" (Univ. of Rochester). READ IN FULL. ~4,800 words.
- Abstract (verbatim, core): "On July 10th, 2019 the Atlantic League had the first game ever called by a robotic umpire. … The purpose of our paper is to measure the impact that the robotic umpires will have on the outcomes of games and individual at bats. … Initially, we identified all the pitches that were miscalled based on a standard strike zone. Then using 'Run Expectancy based on the 24 base-out states' (RE24) we analyzed the impact of miscalled pitches … The results of our study show that for every missed call the run expectancy increase by .065 runs." (The abstract unusually embeds Equation 1 and Table 1.)
- Data: Kaggle "MLB Pitch Data 2015–2018" (Schale), N = 2,867,154 pitches, 40 features. Method: fixed zone (17 in + 0.5 in each side; constant height), called pitches only, ~10% miscalled; RE24 averaged by count ("RE12"); pitcher case studies.
- Numbers: 0.065 runs per missed call (they also write "per game" once — an internal inconsistency); ~3× more incorrect balls than incorrect strikes; miscalled ball +0.12 runs, miscalled strike −0.10; knuckleball called correctly 87.4%, splitter 93.0%; Kershaw 5.4% of pitches called strikes that were balls vs league 2.3% (2018); RE12 by count −0.114 (0-2) to +0.248 (3-0).
- Cites Green & Daniels 2014 [6] — the only prior SSAC cite. Future work (verbatim): "use a more dynamic strike zone that adapts to each hitter's height" and "hitters that are known to have a good batter's eye could be analyzed to see if they are actually very good at determining what a ball or strike is." Limitations: fixed zone, "Players will adapt", tiny Atlantic League sample. Exhibits: Figures 1–5, Tables 1–3 (Table 1 = Table 3, duplicated).
- Relation: our role-perception pillar answers their batter's-eye question directly; the ABS zone (53.5%/27% of height) is their requested dynamic zone; their 0.065 runs/miss is the "stakes" scalar we replace with state-dependent WP.

### 2.4 Chan & Fearing (2013, WINNER) — "The value of flexibility in baseball roster construction." READ IN FULL. ~5,500 words.
- Abstract (verbatim, first + result sentences): "Drawing inspiration from the theory of production flexibility in manufacturing networks, we provide the first optimization-based analysis of the value of positional flexibility … in the presence of injury risk. First, we develop novel statistical models to estimate (1) the likelihood and duration of player injuries …, and (2) fielding abilities at secondary fielding positions. Next, we develop a robust optimization model … Finally, we apply this model … We find that using 2012 rosters, flexibility was expected to create from 3% (White Sox) to 15% (Cubs) in value for each team, measured in runs above replacement."
- Contributions (verbatim): "(1) we develop new analytical methods to estimate injury rates and the effect of changing the fielding position a player takes, (2) we develop a new metric based on a robust optimization model to assess roster vulnerabilities, and (3) we quantify the value of flexibility for Major League Baseball teams in 2012."
- Data: 2012 opening-day rosters, DL data 1999–2011, ZiPS, UZR/150. Method: LP assignment model → robust min-max with budget Γ; two-stage injury model (logit + lognormal); multivariate-normal random-effects fielding model. Assumptions stated in a numbered run: "This model has several implicit assumptions. First … Second … Last … This last assumption is much stronger but is necessary for model tractability."
- Exhibits: Tables 1–10, Figure 1. Conclusion = "Conclusion and extensions" (four future directions). No SSAC cites; analogy to manufacturing (Jordan & Graves 1995).
- Lesson: an OR model + estimated inputs + a league-wide table of team-level values, plus honest assumptions, wins. Our team-level "value of the challenge right" table (WP gain per team, optimal vs revealed) is the analogue of their Table 3.

### 2.5 Banchio & Munro (2020, WINNER) — "A No-Tanking Draft Allocation Policy" (Stanford GSB). READ IN FULL. ~9,300 words. No abstract (economics working-paper format).
- First paragraph: general mechanism (path dependence → drafts → tanking → revenue) with footnoted anecdotes; second paragraph: "Currently, the NBA bases the draft probability lotteries on final rankings only. Our contribution highlights the importance of the dynamic aspects of the repeated two player games … in two different ways. First, we show that any re-distributive policy that relies on final rankings only … can provide incentives for a team to lose … Then we propose a rule which, while not necessarily globally optimal, is optimal for a large subset of incentive compatible rules and is easy to implement."
- Related work is woven into the intro (Gold 2010; Lenten 2016; Lenten et al. 2018; Dagaev & Sonin 2018; Lazear & Rosen 1981; Rosen 1986): "Our work is most closely related to the existing literature on tournament theory." Roadmap paragraph closes the intro. No SSAC cites.
- Method: Theorem 1 (only a uniform lottery is incentive-compatible among rank-only rules); Theorem 2 (R-NTD satisfies NTC/DC/FAIR/PROB and is optimal conditional on history to t*); simulations (N = 3, 60 games; enumerations for 9 games); real NBA 1987 and 1989 seasons (LA Clippers 28.5% vs 14% uniform; Miami 45.5% vs 11%).
- Humility (verbatim): "We do not claim that this rule is globally optimal for the league objective." Conclusion: "To our knowledge, this is the first paper to examine the draft allocation problem using the theory of tournaments and incentives in a mathematical model of team decision making." Broader implications (welfare policy). Code: github.com/evanmunro/draft-policy. Exhibits: Figures 1–2, Tables 1–2, appendix Tables A1–A2.
- Lesson: an impossibility result + an implementable rule + a real-data replay wins even with two seasons of data. Our analogue: a "when the challenge stock is zero, incentives collapse" observation, and rule redesign (retention, tokens) evaluated by simulation and by 2026 replay.

### 2.6 "No More Throwing Darts at the Wall" (2024, WINNER; Other Sports; Paper ID 193939). READ IN FULL. ~5,700 words. No abstract (blind format).
- First paragraph: "Handicap systems are commonly used in sports to create a competitive balance … Handicaps are widely used in many sports, most popularly in golf." ¶2 gap: "the head-start values are calculated using heuristics that are not proven to truly balance competition." ¶3 idea: a "'credit' dart that the weaker player may use to 'hit' a desired region of the board with a probability of one. Players may use these credits dynamically throughout the game." ¶4: "In this paper, we develop a framework to model the game of darts as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with a dynamic credit handicap system."
- Contributions (verbatim): "1. We develop and solve an MDP model for the game of darts with a dynamic credit-based handicap system … 2. We rigorously show that the current heuristic head-start handicap does not balance competition. In response, we develop two optimization-based handicaps … 3. Finally, we use simulation to demonstrate that dynamic credit handicaps create true fairness."
- Related Literature: three named areas ("(1) modeling the optimal strategy in darts (2) modeling skill level and execution error in sports and (3) developing rigorous handicapping frameworks"), one paragraph each, closing "This paper bridges prior work from the above three areas." Cites Kohler 1982, Haugh & Wang 2022, Tibshirani et al. 2011, Chan et al. 2022 (JQAS tennis), Bingham & Swartz 2000, Chan & Singal 2016 (JQAS tennis MDP handicap). No SSAC cites.
- Model: state s = (score θ, credits β ∈ {0..9}, throws left i, turn points u), >1.8M states; 729 aim targets + 62 credit regions; cost 1 per turn; bivariate-Gaussian execution error scaled by ε; policy iteration; "exploit the monotonic structure of darts to solve this more quickly as a dynamic program."
- Findings directly analogous to a challenge token: "the first credit brings the most value … because the optimal policy instructs players to always save their credits for the end of the game"; head-starts shift the mean of the finish-time distribution but not its shape, whereas dynamic credits reshape it → 10,000-game simulation shows credits equalize win probability while optimized head-starts over-correct. Future work: "credits could allow 'do-over' throws instead of providing players with a guaranteed result" (that *is* a challenge); "dynamic credits could also be applied and studied within other sports."
- Exhibits: Figures 1–15, Table 1 (Appendix A lookup for practitioners), Appendix B robustness.
- Lesson/relation: the closest structural precedent in SSAC to our renewable-token DP; cite it for concavity in tokens and back-loading of use, and position ours as the first empirical test of a "dynamic credit" rule against revealed behavior.

### 2.7 "Do Behavioral Considerations Cloud Penalty-Kick Location Optimization…" (2025, WINNER; Soccer; Paper ID 20251395; GitHub link on title page). READ IN FULL. ~6,000 words. No abstract.
- First paragraph: frequency and leverage first — "penalty kicks (PKs) are taken with fair regularity (~ once in four matches [1]) and often constitute high-leverage, or game-pivotal, events … 1-0 and 1-1 are the most common … 17.9 and 11 percent … 6.8% of goals"; then "Herein, we examine whether professional penalty-takers strictly optimize on expected conversion-rate … or whether behavioral considerations, such as 'looking credible' … are also at play." ¶2 hook: Romer 2006 fourth downs, Goff & Locke 2019, Hack-a-Shaq/underhand free throws, corner-three rim-vs-backboard.
- Sections: 1 Introduction, 2 Methods, 3 Results (3.1 classical game → msNE predicts zero correlation between volume and conversion; 3.2 statistical game of chance vs nature → +0.729 predicted correlation; hence H0: non-negative correlation), IV Conclusion. Data: n = 536 PKs, UCL/UEL 2015–2020, 8 goal partitions.
- Numbers: top-partition miss rate 12.88% vs 2.48% bottom; save 5.30% vs 23.51%; observed correlations −0.749 (corners) and −0.997 (middles), bootstrap t = −7.71/−10.85; models explain 85.2–86.8%; Lasso coefficients 1.154 / −0.387; "optimization utility is estimated to be 3.12 times as important to the representative PK-taker on the margin as is behavioral utility"; quadratic term ⇒ risk aversion.
- Cites prior SSAC twice: Ehrlich & Sanders 2024 (NBA shot charts, GAM). Limitations: none formal; acknowledges partition-level n = 8 and bootstraps. Exhibits: Figures 1–5, Tables 1–2.
- Lesson: "theory predicts X, revealed behavior shows Y, quantify the wedge" with a single interpretable number — precisely our "revealed vs optimal threshold" and "9th-inning dump" story. Trap: their GitHub handle (sdsander-syr) de-anonymizes the author — our repo must be anonymized for review.

### 2.8 Melville, Melville, Dawson, Nieves-Rivera, Archibald & Grimsman (2023) — "A Game Theoretical Approach to Optimal Pitch Sequencing" (BYU; Baseball finalist). Intro/related work/§7/conclusion READ. ~10,500 words. No abstract.
- First paragraph: enumerates the pitcher's tools (fielders; catcher framing [2]; repertoire) → "This strategic decision-making process is often referred to as pitch sequencing." ¶2: heuristics ("hard in, soft away"); ¶3: "In this paper, we present a novel approach … modeling the matchup … as a zero-sum game"; ¶4: roadmap.
- Related Work (§2): thematic paragraphs — two-pitch sequencing studies (Roegele, Bonney, Carleton), predictability measures (Arthur, Bock, Healey & Zhao), full-sequence embeddings (Prasad, SSAC 2021 [24]; Yoshihara & Takahashi), pitch value models (Healey 2019, Moore, Bay), RNNs, batter embedding from Alcorn (SSAC 2018 "(batter|pitcher)2vec" [6]); then the closest competitor (Douglas et al. 2021) with a pointed differentiation: "they assume that the batter and pitcher make their decisions simultaneously … That is not a reasonable assumption."
- Key benchmark for us (§7.1): "The batter made the optimal swing decision on 70% of these pitches … these pitchers chose the optimal pitch type just 48% of the time" — an SSAC precedent for reporting a share-of-optimal-decisions statistic. Also lists three "benefits" of an unrealistic optimality assumption (bound on batter, historical closeness-to-optimal, implementable strategy). Exhibits: Figures 1–13, no tables. Conclusion: 1 paragraph, no limitations section.

### 2.9 Melville, Wise, Nielson, Mott, Archibald & Grimsman (2024) — "Optimizing Baseball Fielder Positioning with Consideration for Adaptable Hitters" (BYU; Baseball finalist). Intro/results/conclusion READ. ~9,000 words. No abstract.
- First paragraph: "In this paper, we present a novel approach to optimally position fielders individualized for each opposing hitter. We find evidence that our approach effectively increases outs and decreases runs allowed compared to MLB average defenses." ### 2.11 Berry & Fowler (2019) — "How Much Do Coaches Matter?" (Chicago; Other Sports + Open Source; Paper ID 12549). Abstract/intro/related lit/method summary/conclusion READ. ~10,400 words.
- Abstract (verbatim, core): "Although we have anecdotal evidence that coaches matter, the sports analytics literature has generally concluded that they do not. We present a new method for estimating coach effects, which we call Randomization Inference for Leader Effects, or RIFLE. … Our estimates generally imply that coaches explain about 20-30 percent of the variation in a team's success … baseball managers affect runs allowed more than runs scored."
- Related literature: exhaustive, thematic (succession studies → conditional effects → fixed-effects studies → Goff 2013 dissent), then a diagnosis paragraph: "standard studies … will reflect the composite effects of: changes in coaching quality; mean reversion; and transition costs." Method: 6-step numbered procedure; Stata package released. Power/effect-size simulations (Figs 1–2); endogenous-retention Monte Carlo (Table 7); Belichick hypothesis test (Fig 5). Conclusion: bullet summary + explicit scope statement: "Our goal in this paper has not been to deliver an exhaustive or definitive study of coaching. Rather, we offer a method and associated software." Exhibits: Figures 1–5, Tables 1–7. Lesson: release the method/software; put power analysis and a robustness table in the paper.

### 2.12 Guyon (2021) — "Risk of Collusion: Will Groups of 3 Ruin the FIFA World Cup?" (Business/Soccer). Abstract/intro/conclusion READ. ~10,900 words.
- Abstract (verbatim, core): "In 2026, the FIFA World Cup will for the first time gather 48 men's national teams. … Using groups of three raises several fairness issues, including the risk of match fixing and schedule imbalance. In this article we examine the risk of collusion. … We quantify how often this is expected to happen and explain how to build the match schedule so as to minimize the risk of collusion. … we show that forbidding draws … does not eliminate the risk of match fixing, and that, surprisingly, the 3-2-1-0 point system does not do a better job … A short version of this work has been published in The New York Times [25] and in Le Monde."
- Structure: institutional decision (FIFA Jan 10, 2017, with NYT quote on motives) → the fairness issue → five historical episodes (Gijón 1982; Denmark–France 2018; Japan–Poland; Peru–Colombia; Denmark–Sweden 2004) → roadmap → related literature (15+ tournament-design refs; two contemporaneous working papers acknowledged) → exact probability computations under 3-1-0, 3-0, 3-2-1-0 → schedule design → competitive balance → alternate formats → conclusion ("a terrible step back"). Exhibits: Tables 1–11+ (probability tables), few figures. Lesson: the rule-design counterfactual paper — enumerate rule variants, compute risk under each, recommend. (FIFA later reverted to groups of four — cite carefully as context, not as caused by the paper.)

## 4. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- Zhan et al.'s 0.065 is runs *per missed call* under a *fixed* zone (17.5-in half-width, constant height) on 2015–18 Kaggle data; do not quote it as "per game," and do not compare it to ABS-zone miss rates without saying the zone differs.
- Green & Daniels used front-of-plate PITCHf/x location and operator-set zones (2009–11); Savant's 2026 plate_x/plate_z are mid-plate and ABS-zone-based — never mix the coordinate systems in one figure.
- Rosales & Spratt's run value (0.1189) and leaders are 2010–14; framing values shrank after; cite as historical.
- "Every umpire is biased" is their claim about 2009–11; ours must be a new estimate under accountability, not a restatement.
- Do not write "first paper on X" without the qualifier "to our knowledge" (Banchio & Munro; Chan & Fearing both hedge this way).
- The Darts paper's "save credits for the end" is a *guaranteed-hit* credit; a challenge is a *do-over with uncertain overturn* — the direction of the option-value result need not carry over; derive, don't assert.
- Blind review: no author names, no identifying GitHub handle, no "our earlier work"; the 2025 winner's title-page GitHub URL leaked identity — avoid.
- Abstract rules: <500 words *including title*, ≤2 exhibits combined, four labeled sections; recent winners' PDFs have no abstract at all, so the abstract lives in the submission form.
- Do not claim FIFA reversed groups of three because of Guyon 2021; do not claim MLB adopted anything because of an SSAC paper.
- Two topics in one paper (2017 Bullpen) did not win; keep one question, many implications.

## 5. Open questions in this domain we can answer
- No SSAC paper has ever estimated a decision threshold for a review right in baseball; we can report the first revealed-vs-optimal wedge (the PK-paper "3.12×" analogue: e.g., a WP-equivalent psychic cost of a failed challenge).
- Zhan's two open items — height-adjusted zone impact and whether "batter's eye" is real — are answerable with 2026 per-pitch ABS truth.
- Rosales & Spratt's four-actor split can be redone for *challenge accuracy* (catcher vs batter vs pitcher, selection-corrected), and the framing/challenge interaction they could not observe can now be estimated.

## 7. (a) Abstract template for OUR paper (<500 words incl. title; four sections; ≤2 exhibits) — modeled on the winners' rhythm

## 7. (c) Ten sentence-level style rules distilled from the winners
1. Lead with an institutional fact and a number, not with a definition ("Handicaps are widely used…, most popularly in golf"; "PKs… ~once in four matches"; "over one million pitches").
2. State the gap in one clause with a verb of failure: "heuristics that are not proven," "there has not been a systematic theoretical evaluation," "little knowledge on how much this would impact."
3. One sentence begins "In this paper, we …" and names the model class (MDP, robust optimization, zero-sum game, DP).
4. Give a numbered contributions list of exactly three items, each starting with "We develop / We show / We quantify."
5. Put at least two numbers in the abstract that a reader could repeat at dinner (3%–15%; 0.065 runs; 64% sure; 3.12×).
6. Related work is organized by theme, one paragraph per theme, and closes with a bridge sentence ("This paper bridges prior work from the above three areas"); the closest competitor gets a pointed one-line differentiation.
7. Name assumptions in a numbered run and grade them ("This last assumption is much stronger but is necessary for model tractability").
8. Report a share-of-optimal or captured-value statistic when comparing behavior to a benchmark (70% of swings; 96.8% of value in tennis) — one interpretable scalar.
9. Hedge priority and optimality explicitly ("To our knowledge, this is the first…"; "We do not claim that this rule is globally optimal").
10. End with a rule/policy implication and a scoped humility sentence ("Our goal… has not been to deliver an exhaustive or definitive study… Rather, we offer a method and associated software"), then a code/data link.
