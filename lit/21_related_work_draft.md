## 2. Related Work

**The incumbent practice and its gap.** In 2026 every MLB team holds two ball-strike challenges per game, keeps each one it wins, and receives one more in any extra inning it enters empty-handed; only the pitcher, catcher or batter may invoke it, immediately, and the ruling comes from a Hawk-Eye rectangle 17 inches wide and 27–53.5% of the batter's certified height at the plate midpoint (MLB 2025). Through mid-August, roughly [7,975] challenges had been lodged at [4.3] per game and [54]% were overturned (Baseball-Reference 2026). The tools currently used to decide *when* to spend one are run-expectancy break-evens: Baseball Savant's "Confidence Level" prices every challenge against a fixed 0.2-run token cost, and FanGraphs' first-wave analyses adopt RE288 with an explicit "a run is a run" stance that ignores inning, score and tokens remaining (Clemens 2026a; Oler 2026). Practitioners who have looked hardest at the data concede the missing piece: "it's probably possible to quantify the opportunity cost of losing a challenge" (Baumann 2026), and Oyster Analytics' proprietary break-even dashboard is the only tool that varies that cost with game state (Lane & Riley 2026). No published work solves the challenge decision as a finite-horizon dynamic program with a renewable token, tests players' revealed thresholds against it, corrects role-level success rates for selection, or asks how umpires respond to being reviewable. This paper does those things.

**Umpire calls and their value.** Three SSAC finalists touch called balls and strikes. Green & Daniels (2014) showed with 1.03M PITCHf/x calls that the borderline strike probability falls to about 31% with two strikes and rises to about 58% with three balls, that a called strike is followed by fewer strikes, and that "the ring in which the probability of a strike rises from 10% to 90% is a foot wide" — the ring in which every challenge lives. Rosales & Spratt (2015) split credit for a called strike among catcher, pitcher, batter and umpire (Strike Zone Plus/Minus; 0.1189 runs per strike) and observed that "the strike zone will likely remain the final stronghold of the umpire's influence over the game." Zhan, Gerstner & Polimeni (2020) priced a hypothetical robot umpire at 0.065 runs per missed call under a fixed 18-inch, constant-height zone and asked, as future work, for a height-adjusted zone and a test of whether hitters with a "good batter's eye" actually judge balls and strikes well. The wider umpire literature establishes that errors are concentrated at the edge (Kim & King 2014; Chen, Moskowitz & Shue 2016), leverage-elastic (Archsmith et al. 2025), and responsive to monitoring: same-race favoritism vanished in QuesTec parks (Parsons et al. 2011), accuracy rose with post-game feedback (Mills 2017), and consistency improved while home bias did not (Flannagan, Mills & Goldstone 2024). Our departure is that the 2026 zone is legislated and the truth is recorded per pitch: we can re-estimate the Green–Daniels count thresholds and the Deshpande–Wyner umpire slopes under three accountability regimes, and answer Zhan et al.'s two open questions directly.

**Catcher framing.** Framing measurement moved from zone-map comparisons (Turkenkopf 2008; Marchi 2011; Fast 2011) to hierarchical models crediting catcher, pitcher, batter and umpire jointly (Judge, Pavlidis & Brooks 2015; Rosales & Spratt 2015; Deshpande & Wyner 2017), and to Statcast's shadow-zone runs at 0.125 per strike. Every system agrees that the value of a strike is count-specific — 0.06 runs at 0-0 to 0.54 at 3-2 (Deshpande & Wyner 2017) — and that the catcher spread compressed from ≈98 runs in 2008 to about half by 2025 (Sullivan 2017; Baumann 2026). Under the challenge system MLB books framing on the umpire's *initial* call and overturns as a separate "challenge skill" (Baseball Savant 2026); early evidence says framing and challenge accuracy are roughly uncorrelated (Petriello 2026), that catchers are about ten points *less* successful challenging where they frame best (Andrews 2026), and that only 19% of umpire-stolen strikes were challenged in April (Verducci 2026). CalledThird (2026) supplies a 2025 borderline strike-rate baseline. No one has modeled framing and challenging as a joint equilibrium in which a stolen strike raises the batter's challenge hazard and the catcher's own confidence distorts his threshold; we do.

**Review rights as decision problems.** The closest intellectual ancestor is tennis. Abramitzky, Einav, Kolkowitz & Mill (2012) modeled the Hawk-Eye challenge — a renewable budget refunded on success — as a dynamic program in which a player challenges iff his belief exceeds the ratio of the token's option value to option value plus stakes, and showed with 2,008 ATP challenges that professionals capture 96.8% of the attainable value while systematically under-challenging; Nadimpalli & Hasenbein (2013) and Clarke & Norman (2012) solved the same MDP/DP and derived late-set and behind-in-score aggressiveness. Tennis also taught that review changes the reviewed: after challenges arrived, chair crews cut errors by ≈8% but shifted toward the error type less likely to be publicly overturned (Almog et al. 2025), and line judges made more definitive calls (Bassetti et al. 2019). Cricket's DRS supplies the same structure with a team-held token: batters succeed 34–37% versus fielding sides 20–21% (Davis 2017; Shivakumar 2018), a "use-it-or-lose-it" top-up rule in 2013–17 produced a documented late-innings dump (Bayliss 2020), and home umpires under scrutiny over-correct at the margin (Chowdhury, Jewell & Singleton 2024). NFL, NBA and NHL coach's challenges show falling success by period and strong responses to the cost of failure (Krasker 2006; Burke 2014; NBA 2020; Sun-Times 2020), and MLB's own 2014 replay right showed a 58→39% overturn gradient by game third (Imber 2015). Within SSAC, the 2024 winner solved a darts MDP with "dynamic credits," finding the first credit most valuable and optimal play back-loading their use, and suggested "do-over" credits for other sports; the 2025 winner quantified a behavioral wedge between optimal and revealed penalty-kick placement as a single scalar. Every one of these settings has one decision-maker per side and either no per-event ground truth for unchallenged calls (tennis, cricket) or a binding side-cost (timeouts, penalties). Baseball is the first with three heterogeneous agents sharing a token, hundreds of opportunities per game, and Hawk-Eye truth on every taken pitch, so Abramitzky et al.'s invariance assumption becomes testable rather than maintained.

**Do professionals optimize, and do they learn?** Romer (2006) is the template: solve the DP, tabulate observed choices against it, price the gap in win probability (≈2 pp per game on fourth downs). Fourth-down conservatism persisted for a decade after publication until public tools appeared (Yam & Lopez 2019); tennis players showed no learning across three seasons (Abramitzky et al. 2012); expert golfers stayed loss-averse (Pope & Schweitzer 2011); but penalty takers play minimax (Palacios-Huerta 2003), and pooled tests across heterogeneous agents can mislead (Chiappori, Levitt & Groseclose 2002). Liebman & Mahoney (2017) show that expiring budgets produce precautionary hoarding and a low-quality year-end surge, and that rollover restores value — the vocabulary we use for the ninth-inning dump, with the caveat that late aggressiveness is what the DP *predicts*; the inefficiency, if any, is early hoarding and unused tokens. We follow Brill, Yurko & Wyner (2025) in bootstrapping the optimal policy before scoring any decision as an error.

**Full-ABS leagues and rule variants.** KBO's 2024 full ABS (2 cm buffer, two planes, 27.6–56.4% of height) raised high-zone strike rates from 57% to 80% and cut low-outside miscalls to near zero without immediate batter adaptation (Lee, Han & Ko 2025; Hwang, Kim & Lee 2026); Triple-A ran full-ABS and challenge formats on alternating days in 2023–24 with 3 tokens, then 2 in 2025 (Baseball America 2023–25); NCAA adopts 3 tokens and a 23–58% zone in 2027 (Rudner 2026); the 2025 spring trial covered 13 parks. These are our identification calendar and the empirical anchors for token-count and geometry counterfactuals, in the tradition of enumerated rule design at SSAC (Banchio & Munro 2020; Guyon 2021).

**What is new in this paper.** To our knowledge:
1. The first finite-horizon win-probability dynamic program for a renewable, team-held review right with three eligible agents and extra-inning replenishment, yielding a state-dependent challenge cutoff q\*(state, tokens) and a public per-pitch WP288 table with pitch-level leverage — replacing the constant 0.2-run token cost of Baseball Savant and the RE-only pricing of Clemens (2026a) and Oler (2026).
2. The first estimate of revealed challenge thresholds against that benchmark — by role, team, count, inning and token stock — with the unchallenged denominator observed on every taken pitch, so Abramitzky et al.'s (2012) invariance assumption is tested rather than assumed; we report the share of optimal value captured, the WP forgone to early hoarding, and within-season learning.
3. A selection-corrected, role-level perception model (catcher vs batter vs pitcher; psychometric σ and criterion c by location and state) that answers Zhan et al.'s (2020) batter's-eye question and extends Rosales & Spratt's (2015) four-actor attribution from *influence on the call* to *accuracy of the challenge*.
4. Umpire behavior under accountability, identified with the Triple-A 2023–24 full-ABS/challenge rotation, the 2025 spring park-level trial and the 2026 rollout, net of the 2025 grading-buffer change and the 2026 Statcast plane change — testing whether Almog et al.'s directional shift and Green & Daniels' count-dependent thresholds survive reviewability.
5. A framing/challenge equilibrium linking a catcher's stolen strikes to the batter's challenge hazard and to the catcher's own over-confidence.
6. Rule-design counterfactuals on real 2026 behavior — 1/2/3 tokens, retention on success, who may challenge, extra-inning grants, and KBO/NCAA zone geometry — evaluated by simulation and by replaying the season.
7. An open per-pitch dataset (Hawk-Eye location on the ABS plane, certified-height zone, challenge events and outcomes, WP288/LI) and code.
