## Design dimensions that matter (from the literature)

- **Token count and retention.** ICC cut 3 → 2 in 2009 to curb "frivolous" reviews and ODI 2 → 1 in 2011 to kill "hunch" reviews, then went back to 3 in 2020; the NBA doubled volume and raised success 44 → 59% by adding a second-on-success token; AAA went 3 → 2 in 2025. Retention on success is a partial rollover in Liebman–Mahoney's sense; retention on "umpire's call" (cricket 2017) is the only design that refunds a *marginal* review.
- **Cost of failure.** A lost token (tennis, cricket, ABS) versus a timeout (NFL, NBA) versus a penalty (NHL): the NHL switch shows the elasticity — challenge volume fell ~2/3 and success doubled when failure became a minor penalty. ABS is the cleanest case because the token is the only cost, so the DP has no side-price to calibrate.
- **Who may initiate and what they know.** Coach-only (NFL/NBA/NHL/FVS), captain/batter (cricket), player (tennis), pitcher/catcher/batter with no outside help (ABS). NFL success jumped 40 → 60% when coaches got the Hawk-Eye feed; NFL now allows a challenge designee; ABS teams already restrict roles (Reds ban pitchers).
- **Expiry horizon.** Per set (tennis), per innings (cricket), per game (NFL/NBA/NHL/MLB replay/ABS, with +1 per extra inning in ABS). Every finite-horizon system shows the same period gradient in success (NBA 65 → 39%; MLB replay 58 → 39%; ABS 59 → 35%; cricket top-up 28 → 20%).
- **Deference to measurement error.** Cricket alone builds a tolerance zone ("umpire's call") into the ruling; tennis and ABS treat the reconstruction as definitive (Collins & Evans); KBO's full ABS added a 2 cm buffer and two planes.

## Why MLB's design is the most interesting laboratory

The ABS challenge combines features that no other review right has together. First, three heterogeneous agents — pitcher, catcher and batter — draw on one team-held token from different vantage points and with different incentives (the catcher, in particular, is both the framer who may have caused the umpire's error and the agent most likely to challenge it), which turns Abramitzky et al.'s single-player decision into a within-team agency and information problem and lets us estimate role-level perception with the same ground truth. Second, the ground truth is recorded for every taken pitch, not only for challenged ones, so the unchallenged denominator that tennis and cricket lack (Davis 2017: "The DRS does not capture instances of teams failing to challenge") is observed and AEKM's invariance assumption becomes testable. Third, the token is renewable on success and replenished each extra inning without any side-cost (no timeout, no penalty), producing a pure finite-horizon controlled Markov chain with hundreds of opportunities per game — scarcity binds in a way it never does in the NFL (0.3 uses per team-game). Fourth, the design itself varied within the last four seasons — full ABS vs challenge on alternating days in Triple-A (2023–24), 3 vs 2 tokens (2024 vs 2025), park-level spring trials (2025), and different zone geometries (AAA 2023 51% top; KBO buffer/two planes; NCAA 58/23) — so rule counterfactuals can be anchored to real regimes rather than simulated in the abstract. Finally, the officials being reviewed are the same umpires across regimes, so the accountability response that tennis could only measure crew-level (Almog et al. 2025) can be measured umpire-by-umpire, count-by-count.
