## 1. Domain summary

Comparing the success rate of a discretionary decision across groups is Becker's (1957, 1993) "outcome test": if minority loans default less, or searches of minorities find contraband less often, the marginal member of the disfavored group was held to a different bar. Knowles, Persico & Todd (2001, JPE) gave the test its modern game-theoretic form (equal hit rates across all searched groups in equilibrium when police are unprejudiced) and Anwar & Fang (2006, AER) and Ayres (2002) named its Achilles heel — *infra-marginality*: an average success rate is a property of the whole distribution of risk among those treated, not of the marginal case, so two groups with different risk distributions produce different hit rates under identical thresholds, and identical hit rates under different thresholds. Three responses define the modern literature. (i) The *threshold test* (Simoiu, Corbett-Davies & Goel 2017, AoAS; fast version Pierson, Corbett-Davies & Goel 2018; applied at scale in Pierson et al. 2020, Nature Human Behaviour) jointly estimates a latent risk distribution and a threshold per group from just two observed moments per cell — decision rate and hit rate — using hierarchical pooling across "departments" for identification. (ii) The *marginal outcome test* (Arnold, Dobbie & Yang 2018, QJE; Hull 2021; Arnold, Dobbie & Hull 2022, AER) uses quasi-random variation in decision-maker leniency as an instrument so that IV recovers outcomes for *marginal* subjects; Canay, Mogstad & Mountjoy (2024, REStud) show this is a logically valid bias test only in an "extended Roy" world where the threshold does not depend on unobservables that also move the outcome. (iii) *Skill-vs-threshold* decompositions (Chan, Gentzkow & Yu 2022, QJE) show that when decision-makers differ in signal precision as well as thresholds, hit-rate and leniency comparisons are both misleading (monotonicity fails), and each decision-maker must be placed on his own ROC curve. All of this literature works because — and only because — the truth is unobserved for the untreated (the "selective labels" problem of Kleinberg et al. 2018). Our setting inverts the premise: Hawk-Eye/Statcast records where every taken pitch crossed the plate, challenged or not, so the "risk distribution" the threshold test must infer is directly observed, infra-marginality is *testable* rather than assumed away, the perceived-signal noise (σ by role) and the criterion (c by role and state) are separately identified from the challenge-response curve alone, and the overturn rate becomes a posterior-predictive check rather than the identifying moment. Citing this literature converts referee objection 2 ("58.6/48.5/37.7 are selected") from a weakness into a methodological contribution: an outcome-test setting with ground truth for every unit, in which the canonical latent-variable threshold test can itself be validated.

## 2. Works

### 2.1 Simoiu, C., Corbett-Davies, S., & Goel, S. (2017). "The problem of infra-marginality in outcome tests for discrimination." *Annals of Applied Statistics* 11(3): 1193–1216. doi:10.1214/17-AOAS1058; arXiv:1607.05376. [READ IN FULL, arXiv]
- Data: North Carolina traffic stops Jan 2009–Dec 2014; 9.5M raw, analysis on 4.5M stops from the 100 largest local departments (State Patrol excluded: 47% of stops but 12% of searches); white 2,227,214 / black 1,810,608 / Hispanic 384,186 / Asian 67,508 stops; overall search rate 4.1%, contraband in 29% of searches.
- Method: officer observes latent p ~ Beta(φ_rd, λ_rd) (mean, dispersion) for race r in department d, searches iff p ≥ t_rd; S_rd ~ Binomial(n_rd, Pr(p ≥ t_rd)), H_rd ~ Binomial(S_rd, E[p | p ≥ t_rd]). Identification: φ_rd = logit⁻¹(φ_r + φ_d), λ_rd = exp(λ_r + λ_d) — additive race and department effects — so 3RD latent quantities collapse to 2(D+R−1)+DR parameters, "identifiable when R ≥ 3 and D ≥ 5"; hierarchical priors, Stan/NUTS, 5 chains × 2,500 warmup + 2,500 draws, R̂ < 1.05. Explicit caveat: "Without these constraints, the posterior distributions of the parameters would still be well-defined, but in that case the model would be identified by the priors rather than by the data" (p. 7).
- Numbers: benchmark/outcome tests — search rates white 3.1%, black 5.4%, Hispanic 4.1%, Asian 1.7%; hit rates 32%, 29%, 19%, 26%. Threshold test — inferred thresholds white 15% (14–16), black 7% (3–10), Hispanic 6% (5–8), Asian 13% (11–16). Raleigh: blacks searched more (4% vs 2%) *and* hit more (16% vs 13%) yet face the lower threshold (6% vs 9%) because their signal distribution has "four times more mass above 20%". Posterior-predictive RMSE: search rate 0.1%, hit rate 2.9%. Placebo (day-of-week, season) yields overlapping intervals; robustness to threshold noise up to ≈5 pp SD.
- Quotes: "Outcome tests ... are known to suffer from the problem of infra-marginality: even absent discrimination, the repayment rates for minority and white loan recipients might differ if the two groups have different risk distributions" (abstract). "Suppose that there are two, easily distinguishable types of white drivers: those who have a 1% chance of carrying contraband, and those who have a 75% chance. Similarly assume that black drivers have either a 1% or 50% chance ... If officers, in a race-neutral manner, search individuals who are at least 10% likely to be carrying contraband, then searches of whites will be successful 75% of the time whereas searches of blacks will be successful only 50% of the time" (Intro). "It is easier in the blue group to distinguish between innocent and guilty individuals ... resulting in a higher hit rate for the blue group, throwing off the outcome test" (§2.2). "Search and hit rates are poor proxies for the search thresholds." "Our approach is robust to unobserved heterogeneity that affects the signal, since we effectively marginalize over any omitted variables when estimating the signal distribution. However, we must still worry about systematic variation in the thresholds that is correlated with race" (p. 17). On KPT: "Knowles et al. argue that the hit rate ... is identical to the search threshold" (p. 4); on Anwar–Fang: "cannot detect discrimination when officers of different races are similarly biased" (p. 4).
- Relation: the exact statistical object our role/team/count overturn rates are. Group = role (or team, or count), department = umpire (or park, or count-state), search = challenge, hit = overturn. Their "different variance of the signal distribution" case is our case by construction: catchers/pitchers challenge called balls, batters challenge called strikes; the umpire's error mass differs across those pools and across counts (Green & Daniels 2014). Undone by them: the risk distribution is latent; ours is observed pitch-by-pitch, so their model can be *checked* against truth in our data — nobody has done that.

### 2.2 Knowles, J., Persico, N., & Todd, P. (2001). "Racial Bias in Motor Vehicle Searches: Theory and Evidence." *JPE* 109(1): 203–229. doi:10.1086/318603; NBER WP 7449 (1999). [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- Data: Maryland State Police I-95 searches, 1995–1999, N = 1,582 searches (63% black, 29% white, 6% Hispanic).
- Method/model: police choose whom to search at cost t_r; motorists choose whether to carry; prejudice ≡ t_A ≠ t_W. In the mixed-strategy equilibrium every searched group carries with equal probability, so with unprejudiced police the guilty rate among searched motorists is equal across race *and every other observable c* — this is how KPT sidestep infra-marginality: the motorists' best response puts every searched cell at the margin.
- Numbers: hit rates black 0.34, white 0.32, Hispanic 0.11; black–white χ² = 0.97 (p = 0.33); three-race χ² = 21.59 (p < .001) driven by Hispanics; conclusion "consistent with the hypothesis of no racial prejudice" for blacks.
- Quotes: "we construct a test for whether racial disparities in motor vehicle searches reflect racial prejudice, or instead are consistent with the behavior of non-prejudiced police maximizing drug interdiction"; the test works when "the set of characteristics observed by the police is only partially observable by the econometrician." Caveats: police may maximize something other than hits (drug type/quantity, arrests); test relies on endogenous motorist response.
- Relation: the canonical citation for "equal hit rates ⇔ equal thresholds." Its equilibrium justification does not transfer to us — umpires' errors are not a best response to challenge thresholds in KPT's sense (though pillar 4, accountability, is a weaker version), so equal overturn rates would *not* be implied by equal challenge criteria across roles. Cite for the test; use Anwar–Fang/Ayres for why the assumption fails.

### 2.3 Anwar, S., & Fang, H. (2006). "An Alternative Test of Racial Prejudice in Motor Vehicle Searches: Theory and Evidence." *AER* 96(1): 127–151. doi:10.1257/000282806776157579; NBER WP 11264. [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- Data: Florida Highway Patrol, Jan 2000–Nov 2001, 906,339 stops, 8,976 searches, 1,469 troopers (76.3% white, 13.7% black, 10.0% Hispanic).
- Method: troopers of race t observe signal s with MLRP densities, search iff s ≥ s*(t,r) where Pr(G | s*) = C(t,r). Prejudice ≡ C(t,r) ≠ C(t,r′). Rank-order test: absent relative prejudice, the ranking of search rates and of hit rates across trooper races does not depend on motorist race.
- Numbers (Table 1): search rates white/black/Hispanic officers on white motorists 0.96/0.27/0.76%; on black 1.74/0.35/1.21%; on Hispanic 1.61/0.28/0.99%; hit rates on white motorists 24.3/39.4/26.0%; black 19.9/26.0/20.8%; Hispanic 8.5/21.0/14.3%; all χ² p < .001 (troopers not monolithic); rankings preserved (relative prejudice not rejected).
- Quotes: "The outcome test is problematic in empirical applications because researchers will never be able to directly observe search success rates against marginal motorists ... we can only observe the average success rate ... and not the marginal success rate" (pp. 2–3). "Since the equality of marginal search success rates does not imply and is not implied by the equality of the average search success rates, we cannot determine the relationship between the marginal search success rates ... by looking at average success rates" (p. 2). "Once we allow the motorists' actions when stopped to enter into the officers' search decisions, the infra-marginality problem reappears" (p. 3). "Our test can only detect ... relative racial prejudice and not absolute racial prejudice" (p. 4). "we do not mean to say that our test proves no racial prejudice: our conclusion is simply that no racial prejudice could not be ruled out by the data without making stronger and non-verifiable distributional assumptions" (p. 18).
- Relation: the cleanest verbal statement of average-vs-marginal for the paper's methods section; also a template for a *rank-order* check we can run — the ranking of role challenge rates and overturn rates across umpires/parks/counts should not reverse if roles apply the same criterion to the same signal quality (with truth observed we can go further, but the rank-order test is a nice model-free first exhibit).

### 2.4 Ayres, I. (2002). "Outcome Tests of Racial Disparities in Police Practices." *Justice Research and Policy* 4(1–2): 131–142. doi:10.3818/JRP.4.1.2002.131. [ABSTRACT ONLY — closed access; 157 citations]
- Abstract: "This article assesses the strengths and weaknesses of using 'outcome tests' to assess racial disparities in police practices. An outcome test, for example, might assess whether the probability of finding contraband was higher for whites who are searched than for minorities who are searched."
- Role in the literature (via Simoiu et al. 2017, who cite it for both terms): names the two failure modes — the *infra-marginality* problem (average vs marginal success) and the *subgroup validity* problem (equal aggregate hit rates can hide unequal thresholds in subgroups, and vice versa); also names the "qualified pool or denominator problem" of benchmark tests. Companion: Ayres & Waldfogel (1994, *Stanford Law Review* 46: 987–1047) market test of bail setting [memory].
- Relation: cite for the vocabulary; do not quote beyond the abstract. Our count- and leverage-specific analyses are the "subgroup validity" check.

### 2.5 Arnold, D., Dobbie, W., & Yang, C. S. (2018). "Racial Bias in Bail Decisions." *QJE* 133(4): 1885–1932. doi:10.1093/qje/qjy012; NBER WP 23421. [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- Data: Miami-Dade 93,572 cases (2006–2014, 170 part-time judges), Philadelphia 193,431 cases (2010–2014, 7 full-time judges); outcome = rearrest before disposition.
- Method: judge releases iff E[α | r] ≤ t_r; bias ≡ t_w > t_b (Def. 1, p. 6); Prop. 1: bias ⇒ marginal white misconduct > marginal black misconduct. OLS on released defendants is an average-outcome test and fails: "the average treatment effect identified by OLS will not be equal to the marginal treatment effect required by our outcome test" (p. 10). Marginal outcome test: quasi-random judge assignment, leniency as instrument; IV recovers complier (marginal) misconduct by race; assumptions — first stage, exclusion ("Z_i is uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of Y_i", p. 11), monotonicity ("any defendant released by a strict judge would also be released by a more lenient judge"), and equal complier weights across race (Prop. 4, p. 12; checked via linear first stage). "as our instrument becomes continuously distributed, each pairwise treatment effect converges to the treatment effect for a defendant at the margin of release" (p. 12).
- Numbers (WP version): OLS finds no bias (p = 0.42); IV: release raises rearrest 18.5 pp for marginal white vs 0.5 pp for marginal black defendants; D^IV = 18.0 pp (p. 22) [the published QJE headline figure may differ slightly — verify before quoting]; larger for high-risk defendants (36.5 pp); bias larger among inexperienced/part-time judges; both black and white judges biased against black defendants (Anwar–Fang rank test not rejected, p = 0.36/0.41).
- Quotes: "researchers usually cannot observe which defendants are and are not on the margin of release and, thus, comparisons based on average defendant outcomes are biased if defendants have different risk distributions" (p. 1). Caveats: measurement error in outcomes must be race-neutral; omitted judge objectives; exclusion "fundamentally untestable" (p. 20); discrete-instrument bias ≤ 0.5 pp; compliers ≈ 13%.
- Relation: our exclusion-restriction language in 09/22 (tokens remaining, inning, leverage shift the challenge decision but not the truth) *is* the ADY design; the marginal overturn rate by role is the marginal outcome test. Two differences work in our favor: we observe truth for non-challenged pitches (no need to extrapolate) and can check monotonicity directly; one works against: pillar 4 (umpire accountability) says tokens remaining *may* move the truth distribution, i.e., the exclusion restriction is exactly what another pillar tests (see §4).

### 2.6 Canay, I. A., Mogstad, M., & Mountjoy, J. (2024). "On the Use of Outcome Tests for Detecting Bias in Decision Making." *Review of Economic Studies* 91(4): 2135–2167. doi:10.1093/restud/rdad082; NBER WP 27802. [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- Method: recasts the decision as a Roy model; τ(z,r,v) = detention cost + prediction error λ + taste β; bias ≡ τ varies with r holding v fixed. Theorem 4.1: in the Generalized Roy Model (τ may depend on unobserved v) the marginal outcome comparison "could be positive, negative, or zero" whether or not the judge is biased — logically invalid; and "confounding factors can invalidate the outcome test even if completely independent of race ... as long as they manifest as τ varying with non-race characteristics" (p. 31). Theorem 4.2: in the Extended Roy Model (c, λ, β depend on (z,r) only), E[Δ | w, margin] − E[Δ | b, margin] = τ(z,w) − τ(z,b): the MOT is exactly right. Testable implication (Prop. 5.1): the marginal outcome must be strictly increasing in the propensity score (leniency) for each group. Distinguishes this *logical-validity* problem from infra-marginality: "Much of discrimination literature has focused on inframarginality: average outcomes confound differences at margin with distributional differences away from margin" (p. 4); their concern is "the distinct and more fundamental mapping from marginal outcomes to bias" (p. 2).
- Relation: gives the checklist for reading a marginal overturn comparison across roles as a *threshold* difference: (a) rational-expectations-type beliefs or at least errors that do not vary with pitch unobservables, (b) no omitted payoffs — the challenger cares only about the overturn's WP value (we observe leverage, so payoffs are largely *observed*, but psychic costs of a failed challenge — 22 objection 10 — are an omitted payoff), (c) threshold shifters (tokens, inning) move the criterion but not the perceived-quality mapping. Prop. 5.1 is a free diagnostic: marginal overturn rate must rise with challenge propensity within role.

### 2.7 Hull, P. (2021). "What Marginal Outcome Tests Can Tell Us About Racially Biased Decision-Making." NBER WP 28503. [READ IN FULL]
- Method: minimal-structure framework; β = E[Y* | U=0, w] − E[Y* | U=0, b] (marginal outcome gap, Def. 1, p. 4); accurate statistical discrimination ≡ ranking rationalizable as E[Y* | R, S] − t for some information set S (Def. 2, p. 5); canonical taste-based ≡ race-specific t(R). Prop. 1: taste-based ⇒ β ≠ 0; Prop. 2: β ≠ 0 ⇒ racial bias (broad sense, including inaccurate stereotypes); Prop. 3: decisions are rationalizable by some accurate prediction with (possibly race-specific) thresholds iff the marginal treatment effect curve μ(m,r) is strictly increasing in m. Table 1 (p. 9): increasing MTE + β = 0 → accurate statistical discrimination; increasing + β ≠ 0 → taste; non-increasing → biased beliefs.
- Quotes: "Marginal outcome tests compare the average effects of a binary decision on an outcome between individuals of different races but at the same indifference point" (p. 1); "The test can reject that the decision-maker's ranking reflects some accurate prediction of outcomes" (p. 1); "Distinguishing between racial bias due to racial animus and biased beliefs is challenging, as preferences and beliefs can manifest equivalently" (p. 8).
- Relation: the cleanest statement that a marginal-outcome gap identifies *some* departure from accurate-signal decision-making without specifying the information set — useful because we do not know what the catcher "saw." His MTE-monotonicity diagnostic is implementable in our data with truth observed for all pitches (μ(m, role) = P(call wrong | challenged at leniency m)).

### 2.8 Pierson, E., Simoiu, C., Overgoor, J., Corbett-Davies, S., Jenson, D., Shoemaker, A., Ramachandran, V., Barghouty, P., Phillips, C., Shroff, R., & Goel, S. (2020). "A large-scale analysis of racial disparities in police stops across the United States." *Nature Human Behaviour* 4(7): 736–745. doi:10.1038/s41562-020-0858-1 (arXiv 1706.05678, 2017 preprint has a shorter author list). [READ IN FULL: arXiv + Goel-hosted final PDF]
- Data: ~95M stops analyzed (21 state patrol agencies + 35 municipal departments, 2011–2018; 221M raw). Search rates: state patrol white 1.9%, black 4.3%, Hispanic 4.1%; municipal 3.9%, 9.5%, 7.2%. Hit rates: state patrol white 32.0%, black 29.4%, Hispanic 24.3%; municipal 18.2%, 13.9%, 11.0%. Threshold test (Pierson et al. 2018 discriminant-distribution version, geography nested): state patrol thresholds ≈ 21% white, 16% black, 14% Hispanic; municipal ≈ 10%, 5%, 5% (as extracted; verify decimals against Fig. 3 before quoting).
- Key passage: "Compared to by-location hit rates, the threshold test more strongly suggests discrimination against black drivers, particularly for municipal stops ... this difference appears to be driven by a small but disproportionate number of black drivers who have a high inferred likelihood of carrying contraband. Thus, even though the threshold test finds that the bar for searching black drivers is lower than that for white drivers, these groups have more similar hit rates." Also the textbook example (5%/75% vs 5%/50%, threshold 10% ⇒ hit rates 75% vs 50%). Marijuana legalization (CO/WA, DiD log-odds −0.96/−0.98/−0.69) cut searches for all groups but "the bar for searching black and Hispanic drivers was still lower ... post-legalization."
- Limits (their words): "thresholds cannot be identified by the observed data alone, and so inferences are dependent on the specific functional form of the underlying Bayesian model, including the prior distributions"; within-jurisdiction policy heterogeneity; unobserved offense severity.
- Relation: the at-scale demonstration that outcome and threshold tests can disagree in real data (near-equal hit rates, unequal thresholds) — precisely the pattern to expect if, e.g., catchers and batters have similar overturn rates but different criteria. Their functional-form caveat is the sentence that motivates our "truth observed" advantage.

### 2.9 Pierson, E., Corbett-Davies, S., & Goel, S. (2018). "Fast Threshold Tests for Detecting Discrimination." *AISTATS 2018* (PMLR 84); arXiv:1702.08536. [READ IN FULL]
- Method: replaces Beta signals with "discriminant distributions" — the posterior P(Y=1 | X) when X | Y ~ N(μ_Y, σ) — parameterized by prevalence φ and discriminability δ = (μ₁−μ₀)/σ ("monotonically related to the AUC-ROC"); search and hit rates are normal-CDF expressions, giving 77–143× wall-clock speedups (NYC frisk model: ~2 h → <1 min; stop model: >4 h → <4 min). Data: 2.7M NYC pedestrian stops 2008–2012 (frisk decisions) and ~723k weapon-suspicion stops.
- Quotes: "The key statistical challenge is that an individual's qualifications are typically only partially observed" (p. 2). "The threshold test is robust to unobserved heterogeneity that affects the signal ... However, we must still worry about systematic variation in the thresholds that is correlated with race" (§4.1). Threshold-noise robustness: inferred thresholds fall as noise rises but the group gap persists.
- Relation: this parameterization is *literally* signal detection theory (prevalence + d′ + criterion), i.e., the psychophysical model of 09 §2.13 with the truth integrated out. In our data prevalence (share of wrong calls among opportunities) and d′ (role acuity vs true distance) are estimable directly; the fasttt Stan code is a drop-in template for the latent-risk robustness fit.

### 2.10 Chan, D. C., Gentzkow, M., & Yu, C. (2022). "Selection with Variation in Diagnostic Skill: Evidence from Radiologists." *QJE* 137(2): 729–783; NBER WP 26467. [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- Data: 4.66M VA chest X-rays, 3,199 radiologists, 104 stations, 1999–2015; false negatives observed via pneumonia diagnosis within 10 days of an undiagnosed X-ray (one-sided labels; true positives unverifiable).
- Method: radiologist j sees signal w_ij, diagnoses iff w_ij > τ_j; heterogeneity in *skill* (signal precision α_j) and *preferences* (τ_j); each radiologist is a point on his own ROC curve — skill shifts the curve, threshold moves along it. With quasi-random assignment, uniform skill implies (FN_j − FN_j′)/(P_j − P_j′) ∈ [−1, 0]; the data violate this — higher diagnosis rates go with *higher* miss rates — so judge-IV monotonicity fails and yields "the nonsensical conclusion that diagnosing pneumonia makes patients more likely to return with pneumonia."
- Numbers: diagnosis rate 10th–90th pct 8.9–12.3%; miss rate 0.2–1.8%; skill (corr. with truth) mean 0.85 (0.76–0.93); false-negative cost 6.7× false positive; skill explains 39% of variation in diagnosis rates and 78% of miss rates; uniform-threshold guideline *lowers* welfare; raising bottom-quartile skill helps most; two-reader aggregation closes 35% of the gap to first best.
- Quotes: "Failing to account for skill variation can lead to highly misleading results in research designs that use agent assignments as instruments." "When radiologists differ in skill and are aware of these differences, the optimal diagnostic threshold will typically depend on radiologist skill" (p. 23).
- Relation: the closest structural analogue to our role/player perception model — challengers differ in σ (acuity) *and* c (criterion), overturn rate conflates them, and the optimal criterion is skill-dependent (a catcher with a sharper signal *should* challenge at a different perceived-probability cutoff only if the DP value differs; with a common q*(state) the criterion is common but the *challenge rate* differs). Their "who should decide / aggregate two signals" counterfactuals map onto our "who may challenge" and catcher–pitcher–dugout coordination questions. Their labels are one-sided; ours are two-sided and complete.

### 2.11 Arnold, D., Dobbie, W., & Hull, P. (2022). "Measuring Racial Discrimination in Bail Decisions." *AER* 112(9): 2992–3038; NBER WP 26999. [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- Data: NYC, 595,186 cases, 367,434 defendants, 268 judges, Nov 2008–Nov 2013. Measure: release-rate gap between white and black defendants with the *same potential misconduct* Y* — "outcome-based tests ... can detect ... racial bias at the margin of release decisions ... but cannot detect accurate statistical discrimination or measure the overall extent of disparate impact" (p. 3). Identification: extrapolate released-misconduct rates to a hypothetical release rate of 1 across quasi-randomly assigned judges to recover μ_r; hierarchical MTE model splits bias (π_jr) from signal precision (σ_jr).
- Numbers: gap 6.8 pp (76.7% vs 69.5%); discrimination 4.2 pp (62%); μ_w = 0.346, μ_b = 0.436; 87% of judges discriminate; both bias and less-precise signals for black defendants.
- Relation: their "conditional-on-Y*" disparity — P(decision | group, truth) — is what we can compute *without any extrapolation*: P(challenge | role, call wrong) and P(challenge | role, call correct) are observed for every opportunity. That is the sensitivity/false-alarm pair of an ROC, per role, per state.

### 2.12 Marx, P. (2022). "An Absolute Test of Racial Prejudice." *Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization* 38(1): 42–91. doi:10.1093/jleo/ewab002. [ABSTRACT + partial text]
- Idea: each officer's (search rate, average return) pair sits under a concave "return possibility frontier"; the frontier's slope at the officer's point is his marginal return = threshold, and chords between officers' points bound it (Lemma 1: upper/lower bounds on cost τ from slopes to adjacent points). Variation in *discernment* (information quality) expands the frontier. Finds prejudice against Hispanic drivers and discernment differences by officer race.
- Relation: nonparametric partial identification of the marginal hit rate from cross-decision-maker variation in (challenge rate, overturn rate) — a bounds approach we can run per role using team- or state-level variation before imposing the parametric SDT model, and then *verify* against the observed marginal overturn rate. Nobody has checked such bounds against ground truth.

### 2.13 Kleinberg, J., Lakkaraju, H., Leskovec, J., Ludwig, J., & Mullainathan, S. (2018). "Human Decisions and Machine Predictions." *QJE* 133(1): 237–293; NBER WP 23180. [READ IN FULL, NBER]
- The "selective labels" problem: "We only observe crime outcomes for released defendants, not for those judges detained. This makes it hard to evaluate counterfactual decision rules." Solved by "contraction" on the released pool of the most lenient quasi-randomly assigned judges. NYC 758,027 arrests 2008–2013; algorithm cuts crime 24.7% at fixed release rate or jail 42.0% at fixed crime; judges' unobservables look like noise, not private information; riskiest 1% released 48.5% of the time with 56.3% FTA.
- Relation: cite once to say what we do *not* face: the label (was the call wrong) is observed for every opportunity, so no contraction/extrapolation is needed and any counterfactual policy (DP threshold, "catcher-only" rule) can be evaluated directly on the full opportunity set — with the caveat that behavioral responses (umpires, framing) are outside the label.

## 4. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- **Never read 58.6/48.5/37.7 as perception ranking.** It is an outcome test across pools that differ *by construction*: batters challenge called strikes, catchers and pitchers challenge called balls; the umpire's error mass (and its dependence on count, location, framing) differs across pools, so equal criteria would not produce equal overturn rates and unequal overturn rates do not imply unequal criteria (Simoiu et al.; Anwar–Fang; Pierson et al.). Say "conditional-on-challenging overturn rate" and always pair it with the observed opportunity distribution.
- **Catcher vs pitcher is within-pool but still infra-marginal**: pitchers' challenges concentrate on high-emotion counts/pitches whose true-distance distribution differs; team bans (Reds) truncate the pitcher sample (22 §c.2, Martell 2026).
- **Do not present tokens/inning/leverage as an unconditionally valid instrument.** ADY's exclusion restriction requires the shifter not to move the outcome; our pillar 4 hypothesizes umpires call differently when tokens remain (accountability). Either show the accountability effect is small on the shifted margin or — better — condition on observed truth so the shifter is only needed to trace the response curve, not to identify the risk distribution.
- **A threshold difference is not "bias" or "error."** Under the DP the optimal criterion is state-dependent (q*(state)); under Chan–Gentzkow–Yu the optimal criterion can be skill-dependent; under team policy it can be role-dependent by design. Report criteria relative to q*(state), not relative to each other.
- **CMM's logical-validity caveat**: a marginal overturn comparison identifies threshold differences only if the criterion does not depend on unobservables that also move the outcome ("omitted payoffs": psychic cost of failing, manager instructions, ego on a strikeout). Say so; run their Prop. 5.1 monotonicity check within role.
- **Threshold-test outputs are functional-form-identified** (Simoiu p. 7; Pierson et al. 2020 limits). If we fit the latent-risk version as robustness, label it as such and show it against the truth-based estimates.
- **"Truth for every pitch" needs a footnote**: the official ABS ruling exists only for challenged pitches; for the rest we reconstruct location/zone from public Statcast 9P + heights (22 §c.6). This is partial verification (Begg–Greenes) with a low-noise proxy on the unverified side — the opposite of the selective-labels problem, but not literally complete labels.
- **The pool of "called pitches" is itself endogenous** (swing decisions, framing, count) — fine for role comparisons within the pool, but rule counterfactuals ("batter-only", "3 tokens") should not assume the opportunity distribution is invariant (Knox–Lowe–Mummolo logic; Kleinberg's caveat about behavioral response).
- **Do not quote KPT's equilibrium as if it applied**: umpires do not choose error rates as a best response in the KPT sense; equal overturn rates are not the no-bias benchmark here.
- **Verify before quoting**: ADY headline (18.0 pp in WP), Pierson 2020 threshold decimals, Ayres 2002 wording (abstract only).

## 5. Open questions in this domain we can answer
1. **First ground-truth validation of the threshold test.** Fit Simoiu/Pierson's latent-risk model to (challenge rate, overturn rate) cells by role × umpire (or × count/park), then compare the inferred thresholds and risk distributions to the *observed* distribution of true miss distance and to the truth-based SDT fit. Reports how far infra-marginality moves outcome-test conclusions and whether the hierarchical model recovers thresholds — a methods contribution beyond baseball.
2. **Testable (not assumed) infra-marginality**: compare, by role/team/count, the distribution of P(call wrong | location) over *opportunities*; report the "expected overturn rate at a common criterion" and the decomposition of the raw overturn gap into (a) opportunity-set differences, (b) criterion differences, (c) acuity (σ) differences (Chan–Gentzkow–Yu decomposition; Savant's exp_rate_overturns is (a) only).
3. **Marginal overturn rates by role/team** via (i) the response curve at the estimated criterion and (ii) an ADY-style shifter design (tokens remaining, inning), with CMM Prop. 5.1 monotonicity and Hull's MTE-slope diagnostics; are the marginal rates equal to the DP's breakeven q*(state)? (AEKM's near-optimality question restated as a marginal outcome test.)
4. **Rank-order (Anwar–Fang) exhibit**: do role rankings of challenge rate and overturn rate reverse across umpires/parks/counts? A model-free first pass at "monolithic" role behavior.
5. **Marx bounds vs truth**: chord-slope bounds on the marginal overturn rate from cross-team/cross-state variation, checked against the observed margin.
6. **Team-level "outcome tests"** (MIN 33.6 runs; CIN 0.114 runs/challenge; Baumann 2026): how much of team differences is opportunity set (own pitchers'/catchers' pitch mix, umpires faced) vs policy (criterion) vs skill?
7. **Count as subgroup validity**: overturn rates by count are contaminated by count-specific umpire error (Green–Daniels); estimate count-specific criteria with truth held fixed — is the 3-2 challenge rate spike (9.4%, Martell) a criterion shift or an opportunity shift?
8. **Whose signal is better, structurally**: role-specific σ from the response curve, with the catcher-framing interaction (Andrews 2026: worse where they frame best) as a test of "over-confidence" = criterion, not σ.
