## 1. Domain summary

A large economics literature studies agents — radiologists, obstetricians, ER physicians, bail judges — who make binary decisions from a private, noisy read of a case, and asks whether across-agent variation reflects *preferences/thresholds* or *skill/information*. Its central lesson is negative and general: decision + realized-outcome data (a diagnosis rate and a miss rate; a release rate and a re-arrest rate) put each agent at one point in ROC space and therefore **cannot** separate a low threshold from a poor signal without extra structure — quasi-random assignment of cases to agents (Chan, Gentzkow & Yu 2022; Kleinberg et al. 2018; Arnold, Dobbie & Hull 2022), a case-level risk index that the decision should track (Currie & MacLeod 2017; Abaluck et al. 2016), or an explicit model that identifies the *marginal* rather than the *average* case (Canay, Mogstad & Mountjoy 2024; Rambachan 2024). A second, sharper obstacle — the "selective labels" problem (Lakkaraju et al. 2017; Kleinberg et al. 2018) — is that the outcome of the untaken action (the un-released defendant, the un-diagnosed patient) is never observed, which forces "contraction"/IV designs and bounds. Our setting is isomorphic in structure (three roles + umpire, each with a threshold and a perceptual precision, and a naive success-rate ranking C 58% > B 48% > P 38% that referees will read as "skill") but strictly better in data: Hawk-Eye locates every taken pitch and the ABS ruling is a deterministic function of location and batter height, so the label "was the call wrong?" exists for **every** eligible pitch, challenged or not, and the challenger's decision is observed as a full function of the true miss distance rather than as a single (FPR, TPR) point. Signal-detection theory (DeCarlo 1998: SDT ≡ probit with criterion = intercept, sensitivity = slope; Wichmann & Hill 2001 on lapse parameters; Mueller & Weidemann 2008 on decision noise) tells us exactly what is then identified and what still is not: σ and τ per role are identified from the challenge-probability curve alone, but *unmodeled heterogeneity in τ masquerades as low skill*, and a lapse/attention asymptote must be estimated or σ is biased upward. Umpires themselves have been studied under this design already — PITCHf/x truth on every pitch let Chen, Moskowitz & Shue (2016) and Green & Daniels (2014, 2021) identify count- and sequence-dependent criterion shifts nonparametrically — which is the template we extend from the caller to the challenger.

## 2. Works

### 2.1 Chan, D. C., Gentzkow, M., & Yu, C. (2022). "Selection with Variation in Diagnostic Skill: Evidence from Radiologists." *QJE* 137(2): 729–783. doi:10.1093/qje/qjab048. NBER w26467 (PDF read: https://web.stanford.edu/~gentzkow/research/radiology.pdf; also https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26467/w26467.pdf). [READ IN FULL — WP text; page numbers refer to WP]
- Data: VHA emergency-department chest X-rays, Oct 1999–Sep 2015; N = 4,663,840 X-rays (from ≈5.5 M) read by 3,199 radiologists at 104 stations. Truth for the *undiagnosed*: a pneumonia diagnosis 12 h–10 days after the X-ray (validated: 92.6% of recorded pneumonia diagnoses get a consistent antibiotic within 5 days). Quasi-random assignment of X-rays to radiologists conditional on station × time cells (Assumption 1).
- Model: latent state ν and radiologist signal w bivariate normal with correlation α_j (skill; "Agent j is (weakly) more skilled than j′ if and only if F_j is (weakly) more Blackwell-informative than F_j′", §2.2); preference β_j = relative disutility of a false negative; decision d = 1{w > τ*(α_j, β_j)}. Skill ⇔ radiologist's ROC curve lies uniformly above.
- Identification: with only a diagnosis rate P_j and miss rate FN_j per agent, "two radiologists could produce identical diagnosis rates and miss rates through different combinations of skill and threshold" — the pair is one point in ROC space. Under a *common* ROC curve and random assignment, (FN_j − FN_j′)/(P_j − P_j′) ∈ [−1, 0] (higher diagnosis rate ⇒ weakly lower miss rate); a violation is direct evidence of skill heterogeneity (Remark 2). Then a random-coefficients structural model recovers the joint distribution of (α_j, β_j).
- Numbers: moving from 10th to 90th percentile radiologist raises diagnosis probability 8.9% → 12.3% but *also* raises miss probability 0.2% → 1.8% — "Radiologists who diagnose at higher rates actually have higher rather than lower miss rates" (abstract). Mean α = 0.85 (p10 0.76, p90 0.93); mean β = 6.71 (p10 5.60, p90 7.91). Skill explains 39% of the variation in diagnosis rates and 78% of the variation in miss rates; preferences explain 29% of diagnosis-rate variation and ≈0 of miss-rate variation. Uniform threshold guideline *lowers* welfare; raising skill to the 25th percentile helps; aggregating two radiologists' signals recovers 35% of the gap to first best. Judge/agent-assignment IV designs that ignore skill produce "the nonsensical conclusion that diagnosing a patient with pneumonia (and thus giving her antibiotics) makes her more likely to return to the emergency room with pneumonia" (§4.3).
- Quotes: "Physicians, judges, teachers, and agents in many other settings differ systematically in the decisions they make when faced with similar cases." (opening). "A patient assigned to a radiologist with a higher diagnosis rate is more likely to go home with untreated pneumonia than one assigned to a radiologist with a lower diagnosis rate." (§4.3). "Variation in skill can explain 39 percent of the variation in diagnostic decisions" (abstract).
- Relation: exact template for Pillar 3. Their (P_j, FN_j) is our (challenge rate on correct calls, share of wrong calls left unchallenged); their radiologist-vs-physician joint decision is our catcher-with-pitcher/dugout joint decision; their warning about IV monotonicity applies to anyone tempted to use "challenger/umpire leniency" as an instrument. Undone (by them, doable by us): they never observe the case's *continuous* difficulty; we do (d), so we get an entire ROC curve per role, not one point.

### 2.2 Currie, J., & MacLeod, W. B. (2017). "Diagnosing Expertise: Human Capital, Decision Making, and Performance among Physicians." *J. Labor Economics* 35(1): 1–43. doi:10.1086/687848. NBER w18977 (PDF read: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18977/w18977.pdf). [READ IN FULL — WP]
- Data: New Jersey electronic birth certificates 1997–2006, ≈969,000 physician-attended deliveries, ≈1,000 physicians (≥25 deliveries).
- Model/identification: physician's posterior on patient risk = π₀·prior + π_h·signal, with π_h = D_j/(B_j + D_j) increasing in diagnostic precision D_j. Empirical analogue: physician-specific probit of C-section on a constructed risk index, P(C) = F(θ_j(h + γ_j)); **slope θ_j = decision-making skill** (rises with D_j, unaffected by surgical skill, priors, or fees); **intercept γ_j = practice style** (priors, surgical skill, price), unaffected by decision skill. "Physicians with higher quality decision making are more responsive to new information, and less dependent on prior beliefs." (§3.2). Skill measure distribution: mean 1.033, SD 0.183, p1–p99 0.576–1.491.
- Numbers: +1 SD decision skill → −15.5% C-sections in the bottom half of the risk distribution (−1.6 pp) and +5.5% in the top half (+1.9 pp); "any bad outcome" −15.3% (low risk) / −9.1% (high risk); +1 SD surgical skill raises C-sections everywhere (+16.5% low, +8.7% high).
- Quotes: "Expert performance is often evaluated in a one dimensional way by assuming that good experts have good outcomes." (abstract). "We propose to evaluate physician decision making simply on the basis of whether doctors tailor their decisions to the observable characteristics of patients in the same way as a reference or standard physician." (§2).
- Relation: the cleanest statement that *slope on a case-level truth index = skill, intercept = threshold* — literally the probit we fit when d is observed (DeCarlo, 2.11). Undone: their risk index is a proxy; ours is the adjudicating variable itself.

### 2.3 Kleinberg, J., Lakkaraju, H., Leskovec, J., Ludwig, J., & Mullainathan, S. (2018). "Human Decisions and Machine Predictions." *QJE* 133(1): 237–293. NBER w23180 (PDF read: https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/w23180.pdf). [READ IN FULL — WP]
- Data: NYC pretrial cases Nov 2008–Nov 2013, 758,027 defendants (554,689 in train/test; 73.6% released); national replication 151,461 felony defendants, 40 counties, 1990–2009.
- Selective labels: "We only observe crime outcomes for released defendants, not for those judges detained." Solution = quasi-random judge assignment + "contraction": start from the most lenient judges' released set and ask which additional defendants stricter judges (or the algorithm) would have jailed — no imputation for the jailed.
- Numbers: crime −24.8% at constant jailing, or jail −42.0% at constant crime; riskiest 1% (predicted 62.6% risk) released 48.5% of the time; when stricter judges add 6.6 pp of jailing, only 33.2% of the marginal detainees come from the top 12.0% of predicted risk. Skill vs threshold test: "judges are not simply setting a high threshold for detention but are mis-ranking defendants" (§4.2); a model that predicts *judges* outperforms judges, so their unobservables are noise: "judges respond to 'noise' as if it were signal" (§6); "The defendants the judges release do not seem to have unusual unobservables that cause their observed outcomes to systematically diverge." (§4.1).
- Relation: (i) our one-sentence methods selling point — the selective-labels problem is absent for us; (ii) their "share of marginal actions drawn from the top of the risk distribution" is a threshold-free *ranking-quality* statistic we can compute per role/player using Savant's expected-overturn probability or our own P(d > 0 | pitch features).

### 2.4 Lakkaraju, H., Kleinberg, J., Leskovec, J., Ludwig, J., & Mullainathan, S. (2017). "The Selective Labels Problem: Evaluating Algorithmic Predictions in the Presence of Unobservables." *KDD 2017*, 275–284. doi:10.1145/3097983.3098066 (PDF read: https://cs.stanford.edu/people/jure/pubs/contraction-kdd17.pdf). [READ IN FULL]
- Definition: "the decision-maker's choices — the very decisions we wish to compare the algorithm to — determine which instances even have labels at all"; "we observe the outcome of whether a defendant fails to return for their court appearance only if the human judge decides to release the defendant on bail" (abstract). Contraction requires (a) many decision-makers, (b) random assignment; MAE 6.4× smaller than the best imputation method in simulations; datasets: bail (~9,000 defendants, 18 judges), asthma treatment (60,000 patients, 39 doctors), insurance (50,000 requests, 74 managers).
- Relation: the canonical citation for the name; note they never state the trivial converse (full labels ⇒ no problem) — we should say it explicitly and then qualify with our reconstruction-error caveat (§5 below).

### 2.5 Abaluck, J., Agha, L., Kabrhel, C., Raja, A., & Venkatesh, A. (2016). "The Determinants of Productivity in Medical Testing: Intensity and Allocation of Care." *AER* 106(12): 3730–3764. doi:10.1257/aer.20140260. NBER w19956 (PDF read). [READ IN FULL — WP]
- Data: Medicare 20% sample 2000–2009, 1.9 M ED visits, >6,600 physicians; chest CT for pulmonary embolism; 3.8% tested, 6.9% yield; physician-decile testing rates 1.7%–8.2%.
- Model: "Physicians test if and only if the probability of a positive test q_id exceeds a physician-specific threshold τ_d" (§4); *intensity* = τ_d, *allocation* = whether the doctor's risk weights β′ match the true β. Selective labels handled with a Heckman-type correction, identifying τ_d from the lowest-propensity ("just barely tested") patients, and from the fact that "Doctors who test a greater fraction of their patients are less likely to find positive test outcomes among tested patients" (§3.4).
- Numbers: mean threshold 5.6% (SD 5.4%); 84% of doctors over-test at the margin; misallocation lowers yield by one-third; reweighting comorbidities to maximize yield would raise net benefits >300%; "misallocation is more costly than overuse" (abstract).
- Relation: our decomposition of role/player success into (a) threshold and (b) how well the challenger's decision tracks true miss distance is their intensity/allocation split; their yield-vs-rate curve is AEKM's success-vs-propensity signature and Savant's overturn-vs-challenge-rate leaderboard.

### 2.6 Canay, I. A., Mogstad, M., & Mountjoy, J. (2024). "On the Use of Outcome Tests for Detecting Bias in Decision Making." *Review of Economic Studies* 91(4): 2135–2167. NBER w27802 (PDF read). [READ IN FULL — WP]
- Content: recasts Becker's outcome test as a Roy model, D = 1{E[Δ | r, v] ≤ τ(z, r, v)}, τ = c (cost) + λ (prediction error) + β (taste). Inframarginality: "average outcomes across groups generally uninformative about the marginal cases that may reveal a decision maker's differential standards, as average comparisons confound any differences at the margin with distributional differences away from the margin". Marginal outcomes identified only with quasi-random assignment, continuous leniency variation and an extended-Roy restriction; prediction error λ (skill) and cost c (threshold) are *not* separable by outcome tests alone: λ and c "can invalidate the outcome test even if they are completely independent of race". "writing down an explicit model of decision making is therefore a crucial part of testing for bias" (Discussion).
- Relation: the formal reason a role's *average* overturn rate is not its threshold; our §5 likelihood is exactly the "explicit model" they demand, and because d is observed we identify the marginal pitch directly (P(C | d) = ½).

### 2.7 Rambachan, A. (2024). "Identifying Prediction Mistakes in Observational Data." *QJE* 139(3): 1665–1711. doi:10.1093/qje/qjae013. [ABSTRACT — Crossref]
- Test for whether decisions are consistent with expected-utility maximization under *some* preferences given available information (i.e., separates "systematic prediction mistakes" from unknown thresholds); NYC judges: "at least 20% of judges make systematic prediction mistakes about misconduct risk given defendant characteristics." Relation: the right citation for "revealed thresholds vs mistakes" — with d observed on all pitches we can run a stronger version (is any monotone τ(state) consistent with a player's choices?).

### 2.9 Chen, D. L., Moskowitz, T. J., & Shue, K. (2016). "Decision Making Under the Gambler's Fallacy: Evidence from Asylum Judges, Loan Officers, and Baseball Umpires." *QJE* 131(3): 1181–1242. NBER w22026 (PDF read; umpire section). [READ IN FULL — WP]
- Data: PITCHf/x 2008–2012, ≈3.5 M pitches, ≈1.5 M called pitches, 12,564 games, 127 umpires; truth = location vs rulebook zone; "ambiguous" = within ±1.5 in of the edge (≈60% called correctly), "obvious" = within 3 in of center or ≥6 in outside (≈99% correct).
- Numbers: after a called strike, P(strike call) −0.9 pp conditional on true location; −2.1 pp after two consecutive called strikes (6.8% of the 30% base rate); effect 10–15× larger for ambiguous than obvious pitches (p < 0.001); pitch quality is slightly *positively* autocorrelated, so endogenous pitching biases against the finding. "All reported non-zero coefficients reflect mistakes on the part of the umpires (if the umpire always made the correct call, all coefficients other than the coefficient on the indicator for whether the pitch fell within the strike zone should equal zero)."
- Relation: the existing proof-of-concept that with truth on every pitch, threshold shifts are identified nonparametrically as shifts of P(call | location); the ambiguous/obvious interaction is the SDT prediction that priors/criteria bite only where the signal is weak — we should show the same interaction for challengers (state effects on P(C | d) concentrated at small |d|).

### 2.10 Green, E., & Daniels, D. (2014). "What Does it Take to Call a Strike? Three Biases in Umpire Decision Making." *SSAC 2014* (PDF read) [READ IN FULL]; Green, E., & Daniels, D. P. (2021). "Bayesian Instinct." SSRN 2916929 (rev. Jul 2021) [ABSTRACT].
- 2014: >1 M called pitches 2009–2011, 75 umpires; kernel-smoothed P(strike | x, z) surfaces; borderline pitches called strikes 58% in 3-ball counts vs 31% in 2-strike counts; −17 pp after a called strike; "the ring in which the probability of a strike rises from 10% to 90% is a foot wide"; average umpire "requires 64% confidence to call strike three." 2021 abstract: umpires' decisions "reflect an accurate, probabilistic, and state-specific understanding of their rational expectations — as well as an ability to integrate those prior beliefs with signals in a manner that approximates Bayes rule."
- Relation/number to reuse: a 10–90% ring one foot wide is a pooled psychometric slope of σ_pooled ≈ 12/2.56 ≈ 4.7 in — an *upper bound* on umpire perceptual noise because it pools umpires, counts and pitch types (criterion heterogeneity flattens the curve: 2.12). "Bayesian Instinct" is the skill-vs-threshold ambiguity in umpire form: count dependence can be an optimal criterion under noise rather than a bias — relevant to what accountability should change (loss function, not perception).

### 2.11 DeCarlo, L. T. (1998). "Signal detection theory and generalized linear models." *Psychological Methods* 3(2): 186–205 (PDF read: http://www.columbia.edu/~ld208/psymeth98.pdf). [READ IN FULL]
- SDT with normal noise = probit regression: "the coefficient of X (the slope) gives the distance measure d, and the intercept gives −c" (p. 186); "signal detection models are a subclass of GLMs where the inverse link function g⁻¹ corresponds to a cumulative distribution function F" (p. 189); covariates can enter the criterion (intercept) or sensitivity (slope) separately; d′ and c are both recovered from hit and false-alarm rates when the truth of each trial is known. Relation: licenses estimating §5 as a mixed-effects probit with random intercepts (τ) and slopes (1/σ) by role/player and testing criterion-vs-sensitivity effects of state variables as intercept-vs-slope interactions.

### 2.13 Mather, G. (2008). *Proc. R. Soc. B* 275(1643): 1645–1651 [ABSTRACT; full read in 01]. ## 3. What is (and is not) identified in METHODS §5 — a statement we can put in the paper

Orient d so that d > 0 means the umpire's call was wrong against the eligible challenger (an overturn would follow). §5's model gives P(C = 1 | d, r, s) = Φ((d − τ_r(s))/σ_r) (plus a lapse/attention term 1 − λ_r on the asymptote). Because d is observed for every eligible pitch: (i) τ_r(s) is the d at which the challenge probability crosses ½ and σ_r is the inverse slope — both are identified from the *decision* alone (DeCarlo; Currie–MacLeod's slope/intercept); the ABS outcome is redundant with d and serves only to audit our reconstruction; (ii) each role/player traces an entire ROC curve (FPR = challenge rate on correct calls, TPR = challenge rate on wrong calls, swept by s), so Chan–Gentzkow–Yu's Blackwell ordering is testable directly: if pitchers have *both* lower TPR and higher FPR than catchers, they are dominated (skill), not merely more aggressive (threshold) — CGY's Remark 2 in baseball clothes; (iii) success rate S_r = E[1{d > 0} | C = 1] mixes the umpire-error density facing role r, τ_r and σ_r (Canay et al. inframarginality), so role gaps in S are not skill gaps; (iv) no assignment instrument is needed because we condition on the case's difficulty d itself; the residual assumption is that, given d and pitch covariates, nothing else about perceptibility differs across roles other than what σ_r is meant to capture (vantage). Selling sentence: unlike bail (Kleinberg et al. 2018) or radiology (Chan, Gentzkow & Yu 2022), the counterfactual label of every foregone challenge is observed, so the selective-labels problem does not arise and skill and threshold are separately identified from the full psychometric curve rather than from a single (FPR, TPR) point.

## 5. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps

- "Ground truth for every pitch" is a *reconstruction* (front-of-plate → midpoint propagation, ball radius, listed vs measured height); the official ABS ruling is observed only on challenged pitches. Say "label observed up to measurement error, validated on the challenged subsample"; note the validation sample is concentrated at the hardest edge cases (conservative for agreement) but is decision-selected, so report agreement by |d| band and keep the ±0.5 in coin-flip band (METHODS §3).
- Unmodeled heterogeneity in τ (players, teams, moments, DP option value) flattens the pooled curve and inflates σ̂ — the CGY/Mueller–Weidemann point. Do not report a role σ from a pooled fit; use random τ intercepts, model τ on the DP's option value, and show within-player robustness. Green & Daniels' foot-wide ring (σ_pooled ≈ 4.7 in) is the cautionary example.
- Force the asymptote to 1 and σ̂ explodes: FanGraphs' 9-of-31 egregious calls challenged means a lapse/attention parameter is mandatory (Wichmann & Hill); heavy-tailed ε and lapses are near-observationally equivalent — say "precision" for the slope, not "eyesight."
- Success rate ranking (C > B > P) is inframarginal (Canay et al.); role opportunity sets differ by construction (catchers see called balls that were strikes, batters the reverse; umpire error is directional by location/count), so never compare S across roles without conditioning on d.
- Count is a state variable that shifts the *umpire's* criterion (Green & Daniels; Chen et al.) and therefore the density of wrong calls, and shifts the *challenger's* τ (leverage): count-dependence of P(C | d) is not by itself challenger threshold behavior.
- The tapper is not always the perceiver (pitcher-to-catcher, dugout policies such as the Reds' pitcher ban; cf. CGY's joint radiologist–physician decision): "role" estimates are estimates of the *initiating position's* decision function, not of an individual's vision. Check the 2026 rule text on conferring before attributing.
- Do not use umpire or challenger "leniency" as an instrument for anything without CGY's skill-heterogeneity caveat.
- Kleinberg's 24.8%/42.0% and CGY's 39% are domain numbers; cite them as the size of skill/mis-ranking effects elsewhere, never as priors for baseball. Mather's <40 mm is tennis and decision-selected.

## 6. Open questions in this domain we could answer

1. First field decomposition (à la CGY) of role and player variation in challenge success into skill (σ), threshold (τ), and opportunity shares — and the first direct nonparametric ROC-dominance test between decision-makers who see the same continuously-measured cases.
2. Kleinberg-style ranking-quality statistic per role/player: share of challenges drawn from the top decile of ex-ante overturn probability, threshold-free.
3. Rambachan-style test: is any monotone τ(state) consistent with a player's choices, or are there systematic prediction mistakes (challenging small-|d| pitches at low leverage while passing large-|d| at high leverage)?
4. Criterion noise: tap latency (StatsAPI timestamps) as a confidence proxy — SDT with confidence separates decision noise from perceptual noise (Mueller & Weidemann); also month-to-month within-player changes in σ vs τ (learning: does τ move toward the DP optimum while σ stays flat?).
5. Umpires: does accountability move the criterion (τ by count/previous call) or the slope (σ)? "Bayesian Instinct" predicts a change in criterion only if the loss function changes; challenges alter payoffs only on reviewable calls.
6. Team policy vs individual skill: nested variance components (team > player > pitch) — CGY's counterfactual "assign the trigger to the more skilled agent" translates to "who should hold the trigger."
