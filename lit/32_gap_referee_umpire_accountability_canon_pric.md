## 1. Domain summary

Sports economics owns the cleanest field evidence that officials' decisions move when the officials believe someone is watching, and that "watching" comes in distinct flavours that must not be conflated: **social pressure** from a partisan crowd (Garicano–Palacios-Huerta–Prendergast 2005; Pettersson-Lidbom & Priks 2010, in notes 04), **internal monitoring/technology** (Parsons et al. 2011; Mills 2017, in notes 04), **public awareness/publicity** with no rule or incentive change (Pope–Price–Wolfers 2018, building on Price & Wolfers 2010), **post-hoc public grading** (NBA Last Two Minute reports from March 2015 — Deutscher 2015, Belasen et al. 2025; MLB's Umpire Scorecards from Aug 2020 and the BU "34,294 missed calls" report of April 2019), and **transparency reforms that can backfire** (Zitzewitz 2014). Outside sport, the report-card literature shows that public grading changes agent behaviour through both market and *intrinsic* channels (Kolstad 2013: intrinsic response 4x the profit response) but also induces selection and gaming (Dranove–Kessler–McClellan–Satterthwaite 2003), and Holmström–Milgrom (1991) supplies the formal reason: when only some tasks are measured, incentives on the measured task pull effort away from the unmeasured ones. Together these give Pillar 4 (a) its precedent — publicity alone eliminated NBA referees' own-race bias, an effect its authors call "a new kind of Hawthorne effect" — (b) its theoretical prediction that umpires graded and challengeable on the ABS rectangle will shift toward the graded/reviewable dimension (the Almog-style "less-reviewable error type" shift), and (c) a list of confounds (Donaghy-era oversight in the NBA; COVID crowds, the 2020 Hawk-Eye switch and the 2025 grading-buffer change in MLB) that any pre/post umpire-accuracy claim must survive.

## 2. Per-work entries

### 2.1 Price, J., & Wolfers, J. (2010). "Racial Discrimination Among NBA Referees." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 125(4): 1859–1887. doi:10.1162/qjec.2010.125.4.1859. WP: NBER w13206. [FULL — QJE PDF at users.nber.org/~jwolfers/papers/NBARace.pdf]
- **Data:** NBA regular seasons 1991–92 through 2003–04; box-score player-game observations ("over a quarter of a million", 266,984 in the main analysis; ~13,326 games); referee race coded from press photographs "supplemented by the able assistance of a former NBA referee"; player race from Timmerman (2000), Kahn & Shah (2005) and NBA Register photos (p. 1863). Binary black/non-black.
- **Method:** Fouls per 48 minutes on (black player) x (share of white referees in the 3-man crew), with player, referee and season fixed effects and rich game controls (eq. 1, p. 1868). Identification: "NBA referees are effectively randomly assigned to each game" (p. 1859), verified in Table I and appendix randomization tests.
- **Findings:** Own-race bias: black players receive "around 0.18–0.20 more fouls per 48 minutes played" when the crew goes from zero to three white referees (p. 1870), about 4% of the mean (~4.4–4.5 fouls/48); points −0.40 to −0.48 per 48 min under opposite-race crews (p. 1872); "changing the race of just one referee typically changes the chances of winning by around one percentage point" (p. 1877); an all-black vs all-white crew moves win probability by ~3 pp for a typical team (p. 1860). Conclusion: "The bias in foul-calling is large enough so that the probability of a team winning is noticeably affected by the racial composition of the refereeing crew assigned to the game" (p. 1885).
- **Monitoring context (crucial for us):** the bias survives an environment the authors describe as having a "high level of monitoring… and the high level of accountability for their decisions on the court" (p. 1862) — the NBA already tracked "questionable calls made by each referee" in an internal evaluation system tied to playoff assignments and pay. Mechanism they favour: implicit associations in "split-second, high-pressure evaluations" (p. 1861).
- **Relation to us:** the template for "bias under intensive *internal* monitoring" — MLB plate umpires were graded internally (QuesTec, Zone Evaluation, now Hawk-Eye) for two decades before ABS; ABS challenges add *public, in-game, player-triggered* review. Also the template for random-assignment identification (our analogue: Triple-A crew rotation across ABS-mode days).
- **Left undone / for us:** no within-game variation in scrutiny; no test of whether *reviewable* calls differ from unreviewable ones.

### 2.2 Pope, D. G., Price, J., & Wolfers, J. (2018). "Awareness Reduces Racial Bias." *Management Science* 64(11): 4988–4995. doi:10.1287/mnsc.2017.2901. WPs: NBER w19765 (Dec 2013), IZA DP 7945, CESifo 4675, CEPR 9868. [FULL — NBER/IZA WP text; published abstract via Crossref/IDEAS]
- **Data:** same box-score design as 2.1, extended to seasons 1991–2010 (labelled by start year): original 1991–2002 N=259,014; pre-publicity 2003–2006 N=93,626; post-publicity 2007–2010 N=94,682. Treatment = the May 2007 media wave (front-page *New York Times* — Alan Schwarz, May 2, 2007 — ESPN, talk radio, comments from LeBron James, Kobe Bryant, Charles Barkley and Commissioner Stern) around the working-paper release.
- **Method:** identical specification (black player x fraction white referees; player and referee FE; minutes-weighted); pre/post comparison plus a Chow break test at 2007.
- **Findings (Table 1, WP):** out-group bias 0.182 (s.e. 0.062) in 1991–2002; **0.230 (0.093)** in 2003–2006, i.e., bias persisted out of sample; **−0.0002 (0.089)** in 2007–2010; the change is reported as statistically significant at ~10% (break test), so the elimination is precisely estimated at zero but the *difference* is only marginally significant.
- **Mechanisms examined and rejected:** NBA administrators "denied that the NBA spoke with the referees about the Price and Wolfers study… the study did not lead to a change in referee incentives or a change in the way they train their referees" (WP p. 9); referees who left after 2007 were no more biased than average (p. 7); new hires no less biased (p. 8); mixed-race crew share fell rather than rose (pp. 8–9). Conclusion: "The most likely mechanism… is that the media reporting increased the awareness among referees about their own implicit racial bias" (p. 9); "Our results suggest that public awareness of racial bias was enough to bring about meaningful change"; "racial bias is not a fixed characteristic of an individual decision maker, but rather, it is malleable" (p. 2). Open question they flag: "whether a similar impact would occur if evidence of racial bias was privately shared… as opposed to having it publicly disclosed" (p. 10).
- **Published abstract (2018) is more hedged:** it lists "voluntary behavior changes by referees, player adjustments to new information, and institutional pressure affecting referee conduct" as mechanisms and calls the result "a new kind of Hawthorne effect in which greater scrutiny of even subtle forms of bias can bring about meaningful change" — quote this version.
- **Relation to us:** the cleanest precedent for "reviewability/publicity changes official behavior without any rule change" and the source of the Hawthorne caveat for our within-season learning and umpire-response estimates. Also a template for testing awareness *spillovers*: if umpires improve on pitches that cannot be challenged (no tokens left; non-challenging roles), that is awareness, not reviewability.
- **Trap (see §4):** the post period begins in the season the Tim Donaghy betting scandal broke (July 2007) and the Pedowitz review tightened league oversight of referees; the NBA also publicly disputed the 2007 findings with an internal call-level study. "Publicity alone" is the authors' preferred reading, not an airtight identification.

### 2.3 Deutscher, C. (2015). "No referee bias in the NBA: New evidence with leagues' assessment data." *Journal of Sports Analytics* 1(2): 91–96. doi:10.3233/JSA-150012. [ABS+ — abstract and key tables via SAGE]
- **Data:** the first study to use the NBA's **Last Two Minute (L2M) reports** — league post-hoc grading of every call and material non-call in the last two minutes of games within five points at 2:00, published daily from March 2015. 113 games, 1,229 graded events: 496 correct calls, 17 incorrect calls, 593 correct non-calls, 123 incorrect non-calls (so ~11% of graded events wrong; almost all errors are *missed whistles*, only 17/513 whistles were wrong).
- **Method:** call-level logit of "correct" on home team, superstar, nationality, favourite/underdog, time remaining, attendance — "in-game decision making is matched with reviewed broadcasting video… instead of relying on statistical frequency of calls".
- **Findings:** "no support of referee bias in foul calling" for home, superstar or nationality; weak preference toward underdogs (coef −0.43, p<0.05); home coefficient 0.21 n.s. Limitations he notes: only close endings; the league grades itself; video review may already suppress visible bias.
- **Relation to us:** (i) the L2M is the pure "public report card, no in-game consequence" regime — a natural comparison for Umpire Scorecards; (ii) shows how a grading standard ("clear and conclusive video evidence") shapes the *type* of error recorded (INC >> IC), the same issue our ABS-vs-rulebook-vs-buffer definitions raise; (iii) shows the value of ground-truth-graded call-level data over call-frequency proxies — exactly what Hawk-Eye/ABS gives us for every called pitch.

### 2.4 Belasen, A. R., Belasen, A. T., & Olbrecht, A. M. (2025). "With the Game on the (Betting) Line: NBA Referee Performance in the Last Two Minutes." *Journal of Sports Economics* 26(8): 913–935. doi:10.1177/15270025251369447 (SSRN 5135514). [ABS]
- L2M reports across seasons; incorrect-call rates differ by betting spread: "a significant difference in the percentage of incorrect referee calls in games where there is a narrower betting gap… than in games with a wider betting gap"; reported 23% fewer incorrect calls favouring visiting underdogs and 42% fewer favouring home underdogs relative to favourites. Framed as accountability ("the NBA publishes 'Last Two Minute Reports'… to encourage accountability and a consistent application of the rules"). Use only as evidence that L2M data support call-level error analysis; the betting story is correlational.

### 2.5 The NBA L2M program itself and the public dataset. official.nba.com "Officiating Last Two Minute Reports" (per season pages; archive). [READ]
- Current criterion: games "at or within three points during any point in the last two minutes of the fourth quarter (and overtime)"; assessed plays are "all calls (whistles) and notable non-calls", the latter "material plays directly related to the outcome of a possession"; standard: "there must be clear and conclusive video evidence in order to make a determination that a play was incorrectly officiated" (the 2015 launch used a five-point criterion — Deutscher). Categories CC/IC/CNC/INC.
- **Data:** github.com/atlhawksfanatic/L2M (Robert Dinterman) — parsed L2M reports 2014-15 through 2024-25 (4,600+ games), tidy CSV `1-tidy/L2M/L2M_stats_nba.csv`, ~30 variables (call type, committing/disadvantaged player and team, home/away, period, clock, referee IDs, comments), MIT license; raw PDFs archived for the pre-API era. A ready-made cross-sport comparison set for "post-hoc public grading without in-game review".

### 2.6 Zitzewitz, E. (2014). "Does Transparency Reduce Favoritism and Corruption? Evidence From the Reform of Figure Skating Judging." *Journal of Sports Economics* 15(1): 3–30. doi:10.1177/1527002512441479. [ABS]
- After the 1998/2002 vote-trading scandals the ISU anonymized which judge gave which mark: "The stated intent was to disrupt collusion by groups of judges, but this change also frustrates most attempts by outsiders to monitor judge behavior. The author finds that the 'compatriot-judge effect'… actually increased slightly after the reforms." Framing sentence: "Transparency is usually thought to reduce favoritism and corruption by facilitating monitoring by outsiders, but there is concern it can have the perverse effect of facilitating collusion by insiders."
- **Relation:** the mirror image of ABS — removing outsider monitoring raised bias; ABS challenges add outsider (player) monitoring of the plate umpire's most frequent decision. Cite for "direction of transparency effects is an empirical question".

### 2.7 Garicano, L., Palacios-Huerta, I., & Prendergast, C. (2005). "Favoritism Under Social Pressure." *Review of Economics and Statistics* 87(2): 208–216. (NBER w8376.) [ABS + numbers via Dohmen & Sauermann]
- Spanish Primera División 1994/95 and 1998/99: referees add ~108–113 seconds more injury time when the home side trails by one goal than when it leads by one; the effect rises with attendance (~+20% per s.d.), is absent at half-time and smaller when the visitor is popular/nearby — "referees respond to social incentives". The founding "social pressure" paper; pairs with Pettersson-Lidbom & Priks (2010) and Bryson et al. (2021) (empty stadiums; already in notes 04).
- **Relation:** the crowd is one audience; ABS challenges create a second, better-informed audience (players who can force an instant public verdict). Distinguish "social pressure" (partisan, noisy) from "accountability to a ground truth" (Hawk-Eye).

### 2.8 Dohmen, T., & Sauermann, J. (2016). "Referee Bias." *Journal of Economic Surveys* 30(4): 679–695. (IZA DP 8857, 2015.) [FULL — IZA PDF]
- Survey and taxonomy: social pressure from crowds ("referees might make biased decisions even if such behavior jeopardizes material payoffs"), intrinsic favoritism (nationality/ethnicity/gender), status of players, and material misalignment (career/wage incentives). On monitoring: associations "can monitor their referees only imperfectly"; Rickman & Witt (2008) — home bias "is offset in the period after the introduction of financial incentives for referees" (English professionalization); "Parsons et al. (2011) have shown that the introduction of electronic monitoring systems can reduce referee bias in U.S. baseball" — the same-ethnicity strike effect "can only be found in games where umpires decisions are not electronically monitored", suggesting "referees adapt their behavior consciously if there are monitoring systems". Video review's effect is listed as open ("currently discussed"). Reports Price & Wolfers as "4% fewer fouls / 2.5% more points" for own-race crews.
- **Use:** the citation for the four-way taxonomy and for positioning our contribution as the first *in-game, player-initiated* monitoring shock with objective ground truth on every decision.

### 2.9 Dranove, D., Kessler, D., McClellan, M., & Satterthwaite, M. (2003). "Is More Information Better? The Effects of 'Report Cards' on Health Care Providers." *Journal of Political Economy* 111(3): 555–588. doi:10.1086/374180. (NBER w8697.) [ABS — NBER PDF host blocked by proxy]
- National Medicare data on patients at risk for cardiac surgery; New York (1991) and Pennsylvania (1992) CABG report cards vs other states. Abstract: report cards "may also give doctors and hospitals incentives to decline to treat more difficult, severely ill patients… we find that cardiac surgery report cards in New York and Pennsylvania led both to selection behavior by providers and to improved matching of patients with hospitals. On net, this led to higher levels of resource use and to worse health outcomes, particularly for sicker patients. We conclude that, at least in the short run, these report cards decreased patient and social welfare."
- **Relation:** the canonical "grading induces selection on the graded dimension" result. Umpires cannot select pitches, but they can select *what to attend to* and *how to call the ambiguous band* (e.g., under a 0.75-inch grading buffer vs a zero-buffer ABS review), and MLB can select umpires (postseason assignments). Use as the theory-side warning that a public grade improves the graded number, not necessarily welfare.

### 2.10 Kolstad, J. T. (2013). "Information and Quality When Motivation Is Intrinsic: Evidence from Surgeon Report Cards." *American Economic Review* 103(7): 2875–2910. doi:10.1257/aer.103.7.2875. [ABS]
- Pennsylvania cardiac report cards as a natural experiment: "Information on performance that was new to surgeons and unrelated to patient demand led to an intrinsic response four times larger than surgeon response to profit incentives."
- **Relation:** the mechanism we should expect for umpires — public, precise feedback moves professionals even where pay is not tied to it (MLB umpires' compensation is largely seniority-based; postseason assignments are the main lever). Pairs with PPW's "private vs public disclosure" open question.

### 2.11 Holmström, B., & Milgrom, P. (1991). "Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses: Incentive Contracts, Asset Ownership, and Job Design." *Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization* 7(special issue): 24–52. doi:10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24. [Citation confirmed; the only open copy (milgrom.people.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/1991/09/multitask_principal_agent.pdf) is an image scan — key results below are [MEM]; verify wording against pp. 25–26 before quoting]
- Core results: when an agent divides effort among tasks whose outputs are measured with different precision, rewarding the measurable task diverts effort from the unmeasurable ones; hence incentives are optimally *muted*, and — the sentence usually quoted — the desirability of providing incentives for any one activity decreases with the difficulty of measuring performance in any other activities that make competing demands on the agent's time and attention. Motivating example: teachers paid on test scores "teach to the test" at the expense of unmeasured skills. Job-design corollary: group hard-to-measure tasks in one job (low-powered incentives) and measurable ones in another (high-powered).
- **Relation (formal basis for Pillar 4):** the plate umpire's job bundles (i) called balls/strikes on the ABS rectangle — now measured pitch-by-pitch and reviewable on demand — with (ii) less-measured tasks: check-swing appeals, HBP/foul-tip/catcher's-interference judgments, pace and game management, plus (iii) calls that are *unreviewable in the moment* (no tokens left; pitches nobody challenges). Prediction: attention/effort shifts toward (i); error rates on (ii)/(iii) do not fall and may rise; the error *mix* shifts toward the less-reviewable type (Almog et al.'s Hawk-Eye result is the empirical face of this). Second prediction: two yardsticks (MLB's internal grade on the rulebook zone with a 0.75-in buffer vs public overturns on the zero-buffer ABS zone) create a wedge in the band where they disagree; umpires should track the yardstick with the stronger career consequences.
- Companion cites: Prendergast (1999, *JEL* 37(1): 7–63) — survey confirming agents respond to pay-for-performance and that incentivizing hard-to-measure output is unresolved; Baker (1992, *JPE*) on distortion from imperfect performance measures [MEM]; Kerr (1975) "On the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B" [MEM].

### 2.12 Lerner, J. S., & Tetlock, P. E. (1999). "Accounting for the Effects of Accountability." *Psychological Bulletin* 125(2): 255–275. [Citation confirmed via Crossref; content MEM]
- The psychology canon: accountability improves judgment only under specific conditions — pre-decisional accountability to an audience whose views are unknown and who values accuracy induces "pre-emptive self-criticism" (more effortful, evidence-driven processing); accountability to an audience with known views induces conformity; post-decisional accountability induces defensive bolstering; accountability can amplify some biases (dilution, ambiguity aversion). ABS challenges are pre-decisional (umpires know any call may be tested against a fixed objective standard) — the favourable case — but the outcome is also public and instant, so bolstering (e.g., visible over-signalling of confidence) is possible. Use for hypotheses, not for effect sizes.

### 2.13 Levitt, S. D., & List, J. A. (2011). "Was There Really a Hawthorne Effect at the Hawthorne Plant? An Analysis of the Original Illumination Experiments." *AEJ: Applied* 3(1): 224–238. [ABS]
- Recovered the original illumination data: "Existing descriptions of supposedly remarkable data patterns prove to be entirely fictional", though subtler evidence of a Hawthorne-type response exists. Cite when using "Hawthorne effect" as shorthand (as PPW do) — say "scrutiny effect" and note the original evidence is weak.

### 2.14 Abbate, C., Cross, J., & Uhrig, R. (2025). "Video assistant referee and home field advantage: Implications for referee bias." *Southern Economic Journal* 91(3): 1176–1196. doi:10.1002/soej.12731 (SSRN 4295203, 2022). [ABS]
- Top leagues in 16 countries, 2009–2019, VAR adoption: "the implementation of VAR had negligible effects on home field advantage and various crucial match statistics" despite fewer offsides and yellow cards. Counterweight to Holder et al. (already in our notes): reviewability does not mechanically remove bias; effects depend on which decisions are reviewable (multitask again). Related recent SSRN: Scoppa (2026) "VAR, Referee Bias, and Home Advantage"; Kemper (2026) "When the Crowd Came Back" (NCAA basketball).

### 2.15 Public grading shocks in MLB before ABS: Umpire Scorecards and the BU report. [READ — umpscorecards.com/about, /info; bu.edu article]
- **Umpire Scorecards** (Ethan Singer, founder/technical director, now an MIT PhD student; Ethan Schwartz, business director): started on Reddit in October 2019 (postseason); "In August of 2020, we created an automated bot to Tweet out Umpire Scorecards for every game, every day"; filterable website July 2021; "We added historical umpire data going back to 2015" (2022); own API for filtered queries; metrics per game and per umpire: accuracy, expected accuracy, accuracy above expected, consistency, favor, total run impact (RE-based; e.g., "umpire cost the San Francisco Giants 1.85 expected runs"); classification uses Statcast plate_x/plate_z with batter-specific sz_top/sz_bot ("values that reflect the size of the zone once adjusted for batter height and stance"), called pitches only. Mission: "measuring the accuracy, consistency, and favor of MLB umpires". No peer-reviewed study of its debut as an accountability shock was found (Crossref/IDEAS/OpenAlex queries; note that search was constrained) — this is a gap we can fill or at least name.
- **Williams, M. T. (Boston University), "MLB Umpires Missed 34,294 Ball-Strike Calls in 2018. Bring on Robo-umps?" BU Today, April 8, 2019.** 2008–2018, >4 million called pitches; 2018: 34,294 incorrect calls (~14/game), error rate 9.21% vs 16.4% in 2008 (11-yr avg 12.78%); 55 games ended on an incorrect call (39 in 2017); two-strike counts: 29% of the relevant called strikes were balls vs 15% at other counts; top-10 umpires' bad-call ratio 8.94% (avg age 33, 2.7 yrs) vs bottom-10 13.96% (avg age 56, 20.6 yrs); named individuals (Joe West ~21 incorrect calls/game, Ángel Hernández ~19, Jerry Layne 14.18% BCR; John Libka 7.59%). This is a *named-individual* public shock 16 months before the Umpire Scorecards bot — usable as a treatment for a named-vs-unnamed umpire difference-in-differences.
- **Grading-regime facts to hold fixed (cross-ref notes 04/05/06):** internal QuesTec (2001–08) → Zone Evaluation (2009–) → Hawk-Eye; the 2020–24 CBA (Dec 2019) committed umpires to cooperate with ABS; the CBA ratified Dec 2024 cut the internal grading buffer from 2 in to 0.75 in for 2025; postseason assignments depend on evaluations.

## 4. Claims we must NOT overclaim / traps
- **"Publicity alone eliminated bias" (PPW) is the authors' preferred reading, not a clean identification.** The post period starts with the 2007–08 season — the Donaghy scandal broke July 2007 and the league tightened referee oversight (Pedowitz review, 2008); the NBA also publicly contested the 2007 findings with an internal study. PPW's published abstract itself lists "institutional pressure" as a mechanism. Report the coefficients (0.230 → −0.0002) and the marginal significance of the change; do not write "awareness alone".
- **Do not conflate five things:** crowd social pressure, internal monitoring/technology, public post-hoc grading, in-game reviewability, and pay incentives. ABS challenges = public + in-game + player-triggered + objective standard, with pay only indirectly (postseason assignments). Our AAA identification (full-ABS vs challenge days, same umpires) isolates *in-game reviewability*; the 2026 rollout mixes all five.
- **MLB pre-ABS accuracy trends are contaminated by public-grading and measurement shocks that coincide:** BU report (Apr 2019), Umpire Scorecards bot (Aug 2020), COVID short season with no crowds (2020), Statcast's TrackMan→Hawk-Eye switch (2020), 2020–24 CBA (Dec 2019), pitch clock (2023), grading-buffer cut 2 in→0.75 in (2025), and Savant's zone redefinition (2026). Any 2020 or 2025 "break" is not attributable to a single cause.
- **Grading standards define error type.** L2M's "clear and conclusive" standard yields INC>>IC; MLB's buffer yields "accuracy" of 96–98% while zero-buffer Statcast/ABS gives ~92–94%. Never compare error rates across grading definitions.
- **Multitask predictions concern effort we cannot observe.** Phrase results as "consistent with attention reallocation", and pre-specify the non-graded outcomes we test (check-swing/HBP/interference calls, pace, ejections, calls when tokens are exhausted).
- **Report-card "selection" does not transfer literally**: umpires cannot choose pitches; the analogue is attention and threshold placement in the ambiguous band, plus league-level assignment selection. Do not claim welfare losses à la Dranove without evidence.
- **Hawthorne effect:** the original evidence is "entirely fictional" in its popular telling (Levitt & List); use "scrutiny effect" or "awareness effect" and cite PPW's phrase precisely.
- **Zitzewitz's perverse result depends on insider collusion**; do not generalize to umpires, but do not assume transparency's sign either.
- **Random assignment** is PW's identification; MLB crews rotate on fixed schedules (not random but plausibly exogenous to ABS mode in AAA); test balance, do not assert.

## 5. Open questions we could answer
1. **Within-game accountability variation:** are umpires more accurate (or differently biased) on pitches that are challengeable (a side still holds a token) than when the relevant side has none — a within-umpire, within-game contrast that mirrors the L2M eligibility-threshold logic and separates *reviewability* from *awareness* (PPW's spillover) — awareness predicts no gap; reviewability predicts one.
2. **Named-umpire DiD around public shocks:** umpires named in the BU report (April 2019) or in viral Umpire Scorecards posts vs unnamed peers, before/after, holding measurement fixed (re-grade all seasons with one zone definition from raw Statcast).
3. **Multitask/error-mix shift:** under ABS (AAA 2023–24 rotation; MLB 2026), does the ratio of "ball called strike" to "strike called ball" errors move toward the type less likely to be challenged given who challenges (batters vs pitcher/catcher), and do non-graded decisions (check swings, HBP, interference) change?
4. **Two yardsticks:** in the band where MLB's 0.75-in buffer and the zero-buffer ABS zone disagree, do 2026 calls track the internal grade or the public overturn risk?
5. **Public grading as a pre-ABS treatment:** does an umpire's next-game accuracy respond to the engagement/virality of his previous scorecard (a dose–response version of PPW)?
6. **Cross-sport report-card comparison:** L2M (public grade, no consequence, close endings only) vs Umpire Scorecards (public grade, every game) vs ABS challenge (public grade + instant reversal): compare error trends around each launch with the same event-study template — a Sloan-friendly figure.
